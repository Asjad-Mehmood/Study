This chapter is about the energy locked in nuclei: mass defect and binding energy, fission and fusion, nuclear reactors, radioactive decay and half-life, tracers in medicine, and annihilation. The one idea behind it all is E = mc²: a little lost mass becomes a lot of energy.

**How to learn this chapter (about 3 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** mass defect, binding energy, the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve. Memory hook: "iron sits at the top; heavy nuclei split down to it (fission), light nuclei join up to it (fusion)".
2. **Session 2:** fission, chain reaction, critical mass, the reactor and its parts, fusion and why it is hard.
3. **Session 3:** activity, decay constant, half-life (N = N₀(½)ⁿ), tracers, annihilation. Then MCQs and the 6 numericals.

### 1. Key ideas

**The nucleus**: Rutherford (1911) found a tiny, dense, positive nucleus. Protons were proposed in 1920 and Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932. A nuclide is written ᴬ\_Z X: Z = number of protons (atomic number), A = number of nucleons (mass number), N = A − Z = number of neutrons. A nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁴ m across; an atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m.

**Mass defect (Δm)**: a nucleus always weighs **less** than its separate protons and neutrons added together. The difference is the mass defect: Δm = Zm\_p + (A − Z)m\_n − M\_nucleus. The missing mass became energy when the nucleus formed.

**Binding energy (B.E.)**: the work needed to break a nucleus completely into separate protons and neutrons: B.E. = Δm c². Masses are often in atomic mass units: 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, equivalent to 931.5 MeV. Helium-4 has B.E. about 27–28 MeV.

**Binding energy per nucleon** (B.E./A) shows how tightly each nucleon is held, i.e. how stable a nucleus is.

- It rises steeply from hydrogen to helium, then up to a peak of about 8.7 MeV per nucleon at iron-56, the most stable nucleus. The most stable region is A ≈ 50–80.
- It then falls slowly for heavy nuclei (uranium ≈ 7.6 MeV per nucleon).
- So both very light and very heavy nuclei are less tightly bound. Energy is released by any reaction that moves the products up the curve: heavy nuclei splitting (**fission**) or light nuclei joining (**fusion**).

**Nuclear fission** (Hahn and Strassmann): a slow neutron hits uranium-235, which splits into two medium nuclei of roughly equal size (e.g. barium-141 and krypton-92), plus 2–3 fast neutrons and about **200 MeV** of energy. The products have B.E. ≈ 8.5 MeV per nucleon versus 7.6 for uranium, so about 0.9 MeV per nucleon × 235 nucleons ≈ 211 MeV is released. The fragments vary from fission to fission and are radioactive. U-235 and Pu-239 fission most easily with slow neutrons.

**Chain reaction**: the 2–3 neutrons from one fission can cause more fissions, which release more neutrons, and so on.

- **Critical mass**: the smallest mass of fissile material in which, on average, one neutron from each fission causes another fission, so the reaction just sustains itself.
- Less than critical mass: too many neutrons escape, and the chain dies out.
- Much more than critical mass: the chain grows explosively. This is an **uncontrolled** chain reaction (atom bomb).
- Exactly one neutron per fission causing the next fission: a steady, **controlled** chain reaction (nuclear reactor).

**Nuclear reactor (water-moderated power reactor)**: works like a thermal power station, but fission heats the water instead of burning coal. Heat → steam → turbine → generator → electricity.

| Part | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Core and fuel rods | Hold the fuel: uranium enriched to about 2.4–3% U-235 (also Pu-239 or U-233) |
| Moderator (water, heavy water or graphite) | Slows fast fission neutrons to slow (thermal) neutrons, which cause fission far better |
| Control rods (cadmium or boron) | Absorb neutrons; moved in or out to keep exactly one neutron per fission causing the next |
| Safety rods | Drop fully into the core in an emergency to stop the chain reaction |
| Coolant (water or heavy water under high pressure) | Carries heat (core at about 500 °C) to the heat exchanger |
| Heat exchanger | Turns ordinary water into high-pressure steam for the turbine |
| Shielding (thick steel and concrete) | Stops radiation escaping |

**Why uranium must be enriched**: natural uranium has only 0.7% U-235, the part that fissions with slow neutrons. The rest, U-238, absorbs neutrons without fission. With so little U-235, the chain reaction cannot keep going, so the U-235 share is raised to about 3%.

**Pakistan's nuclear power**: plants at Karachi (KANUPP, which used heavy water as moderator and coolant, cooled by sea water) and Chashma (Mianwali, cooled by Indus water). The book gives a total capacity of more than 3000 MW, from pressurized water reactors.

**Nuclear waste**: spent fuel stays strongly radioactive for thousands of years (plutonium's half-life is about 24,000 years). It must be kept away from soil, air and drinking water. The safest storage found so far is deep in dry, old salt mines.

**Nuclear vs chemical energy**: one fission releases about 200 MeV, while one chemical reaction (burning) releases only a few eV. So 1 kg of U-235 gives roughly 8 × 10¹³ J, while 1 kg of coal gives about 3 × 10⁷ J: millions of times less.

**Nuclear fusion**: two light nuclei join to make a heavier one, losing mass and releasing energy, e.g. ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV. Fusion gives more energy per nucleon than fission. But it is very hard to start: the positive nuclei repel strongly, so they must collide at huge speeds. That needs temperatures of about 10 million °C (a **thermonuclear** reaction). So far it happens naturally in the Sun and stars, in hydrogen bombs (triggered by an atom bomb), and in experimental reactors (China's EAST tokamak, the "artificial Sun"). Controlled fusion power is not yet available.

**Radioactive decay is random and spontaneous**: we can never say when a particular nucleus will decay. The count rate on a detector fluctuates, which is evidence of this randomness. But a large sample follows a smooth pattern.

- **Activity**: the number of decays per second. Unit: becquerel (Bq) = 1 decay per second. A detector's count rate is proportional to the activity.
- **Decay constant (λ)**: the fraction of nuclei that decay per second. ΔN = −λNΔt, so activity A = λN. Unit: s⁻¹. A larger λ means faster decay.
- **Half-life (T½)**: the time for half of the radioactive nuclei (or the activity) to decay. After n half-lives, (½)ⁿ of the sample remains. A sample never decays completely. T½ = 0.693/λ.
- Examples: U-238, 4.5 × 10⁹ years; radium-226, 1620 years; radon, 3.8 days; U-239, 23.5 minutes.
- Decay is **exponential**: N = N₀e^(−λt). The same equation works for activity and count rate.
- Stable elements can be made radioactive by bombarding them with high-energy particles (artificial radioactivity).

**Radioactive tracers**: a radioactive isotope behaves chemically just like the normal one, but its radiation can be detected, so it lets us follow a substance through a process.

- **Medicine**: iodine-131 checks the thyroid (an overactive gland absorbs more than twice the normal amount) and treats thyroid cancer; sodium-24 studies blood circulation; technetium-99m is used in imaging; phosphorus-32 or strontium-90 treat skin cancer; radioactive "seeds" are implanted in tumours. Doses must be controlled because radiation also harms healthy cells.
- **Agriculture**: tracers in fertilizer show how much a plant absorbs; radiation-induced mutations have produced better rice, wheat, chickpea and cotton varieties.
- **Research and industry**: following chemical and biological reactions.

**Annihilation**: when a particle meets its antiparticle, both vanish and their mass becomes gamma-ray energy. Mass-energy and momentum are both conserved. An electron and positron give 2m\_ec² = 1.02 MeV, as two 0.51 MeV gamma photons moving in opposite directions. These photons pass out of the body and can be detected (the basis of PET scans). Particle colliders such as CERN's LHC smash particles at TeV energies; they found the W and Z bosons predicted by Prof. Abdus Salam's electroweak theory, and the Higgs boson (July 2012).

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Half-life and decay constant: T½ = 0.693/λ**

1. The number of nuclei left after time t is N = N₀e^(−λt).
2. At t = T½, N = ½N₀:

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}N_0 = N_0e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} \Rightarrow e^{\lambda T_{1/2}} = 2 \Rightarrow \lambda T_{1/2} = \ln 2 = 0.693
$$

$$
T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}
$$

**(b) Relation between number of half-lives and time**

1. Start with N₀ nuclei. After 1 half-life, N₀/2 remain. After 2, N₀/4 = (½)²N₀. After 3, (½)³N₀.
2. After n half-lives:

$$
N = N_0\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^n \qquad n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} \qquad \text{decayed} = N_0 - N
$$

3. Example: after 3 half-lives, 1/8 remains and 7/8 has decayed.

**(c) Energy from fission using the B.E. curve**: uranium has about 7.6 MeV per nucleon; the fragments have about 8.5 MeV per nucleon. Each nucleon becomes 0.9 MeV more tightly bound, so the energy released per fission ≈ 0.9 × 235 ≈ 211 MeV (about 200 MeV).

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**19.1 Define binding energy. How is it calculated?** The energy needed to break a nucleus into its separate protons and neutrons (equal to the energy released when the nucleus formed). Find the mass defect Δm = Zm\_p + (A − Z)m\_n − M, then B.E. = Δm c² (or Δm in u × 931.5 MeV).

**19.2 "If the probability of decay per unit time is doubled, the half-life is also doubled." Comment.** The statement is wrong. The probability of decay per unit time is the decay constant λ, and T½ = 0.693/λ. Doubling λ **halves** the half-life: the substance decays faster.

**19.3 What percentage remains after four half-lives?** (½)⁴ = 1/16 = **6.25%**.

**19.4 Why is natural uranium a low-grade nuclear fuel?** Only 0.7% of it is U-235, which fissions with slow neutrons. The 99.3% U-238 mostly absorbs neutrons without fission. This is too little U-235 to sustain a chain reaction, so it must be enriched to about 3%.

**19.5 Give one medical use of a radioactive tracer.** Iodine-131 is given to a patient; a detector over the neck measures how much the thyroid gland absorbs, showing whether it is working normally (an overactive gland absorbs more than twice the normal amount).

**19.6 How is energy generated in the Sun?** By nuclear fusion in its core, at about 10⁷ °C. Hydrogen nuclei fuse step by step into helium. The helium formed has less mass than the hydrogen that made it, and this mass becomes energy (E = mc²), released as light and heat.

**19.7 What is half-life?** The time in which half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample decay (or the activity falls to half). Each radioactive isotope has its own fixed half-life.

**19.8 Controlled vs uncontrolled chain reaction.** Controlled: control rods absorb extra neutrons so that, on average, only one neutron from each fission causes another; energy is released steadily (nuclear reactor). Uncontrolled: more than one neutron per fission causes new fissions, so the number of fissions grows explosively in a tiny time (atom bomb).

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**19.1 How is a nuclear power station different from a thermal (fossil-fuel) power station?**

|  | Nuclear power station | Thermal power station |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat source | Fission of uranium in a reactor | Burning coal, oil or gas in a furnace |
| Fuel needed | Very little (fuel lasts months) | Huge amounts, burned continuously |
| Air pollution | No smoke or CO₂ while running | Smoke, CO₂ and other gases |
| Waste | Small amount, but radioactive for thousands of years | Large amounts of ash and gases |
| Extra needs | Moderator, control rods, heavy shielding | None of these |

Both turn heat into steam, which drives a turbine and generator.

**19.2 Advantages of fusion reactors over fission reactors.** The fuel (deuterium) is abundant in sea water; fusion gives more energy per kg of fuel; its main product, helium, is not radioactive, so there is little long-lived waste; there is no runaway chain reaction (if conditions fail, it simply stops); and it produces no material for bombs.

**19.3 Activity drops by 10% in a year. What will it be after another year?** Decay is exponential, so the same **fraction** decays each year: each year the activity is multiplied by 0.9. After 2 years it is 0.9 × 0.9 = **0.81** of the original (it falls by 9% of the original in the second year, not another 10%).

**19.4 Activity 10 × 10⁴ Bq, half-life 20 min. Activity after one hour?** 60 min = 3 half-lives, so the activity falls to (½)³ = 1/8: 10 × 10⁴ / 8 = **1.25 × 10⁴ Bq**.

**19.5 What makes fusion hard to achieve?** The nuclei are positive and repel strongly, so they need enormous speeds, i.e. temperatures of about 10⁷ °C or more. No material container can hold such hot plasma, so it must be held by strong magnetic fields (tokamak). The plasma must be kept dense and hot long enough for fusion to happen, and so far more energy goes in than comes out.

**19.6 How is a nuclear reaction different from a chemical reaction?** A nuclear reaction changes the nucleus, so one element becomes another; a chemical reaction only rearranges outer electrons, and the elements stay the same. Nuclear energy per reaction is millions of times larger (MeV vs eV), with a measurable mass change. Nuclear reactions are not affected by temperature, pressure or chemical combination.

**19.7 Why do boron rods get hot when absorbing neutrons?** Boron-10 captures a neutron and splits into lithium-7 and an alpha particle: ¹⁰B + n → ⁷Li + ⁴He. These products fly apart with kinetic energy (about 2.8 MeV). As they collide with atoms in the rod, this K.E. turns into heat.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**19.1 Mass defect and binding energy of deuterium.** m\_p = 1.6726 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, m\_n = 1.6749 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, M = 3.3435 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

$$
\Delta m = (1.6726 + 1.6749 - 3.3435)\times10^{-27} = 4.0\times10^{-30}\ \text{kg}
$$

$$
B.E. = \Delta m\,c^2 = (4.0\times10^{-30})(3\times10^{8})^2 = 3.6\times10^{-13}\ \text{J} = \frac{3.6\times10^{-13}}{1.6\times10^{-13}} = 2.25\ \text{MeV}
$$

(The book gives 2.23 MeV; the small difference comes from rounding the masses.)

**19.2 Half-life of strontium (λ = 1.99 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹).**

$$
T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{1.99\times10^{-5}} = 3.48\times10^{4}\ \text{s} = \frac{3.48\times10^{4}}{3600} = 9.7\ \text{hours}
$$

**19.3 Krypton left after 12.64 minutes.** T½ = 3.16 min, so n = 12.64/3.16 = 4 half-lives.

$$
m = 40\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \frac{40}{16} = 2.5\ \text{g}
$$

**19.4 Half-life from 18 years of decay.** The question as printed leaves out the starting amount. The book's answer (3 years) means 18 years = 6 half-lives, i.e. the sample fell to (½)⁶ = 1/64 of its start, for example from 32 g to 0.5 g. Method: find the fraction left, write it as (½)ⁿ, then T½ = t/n = 18/6 = **3 years**.

**19.5 Speed of a 5.56 MeV alpha particle.** E = 5.56 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹³ = 8.90 × 10⁻¹³ J; m\_α = 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(8.90\times10^{-13})}{6.64\times10^{-27}}} = 1.64\times10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

(The book prints 1.68 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹; with the standard alpha mass the answer is 1.64 × 10⁷.)

**19.6 Energy released in ⁷Li + ¹H → 2 ⁴He.**

$$
\Delta m = (7.01823 + 1.00814) - 2(4.00388) = 0.01861\ \text{u}
$$

$$
E = 0.01861 \times 931.5 = 17.3\ \text{MeV}
$$

The book prints 19.2 MeV, but the masses given lead to 17.3 MeV (which also matches the measured value of this reaction). Check with your teacher.

**Book example to practise (americium-241):** 5.1 μg contains N = (5.1 × 10⁻⁶ / 241) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.27 × 10¹⁶ nuclei. With activity 5.9 × 10⁵ Bq, λ = A/N = 4.6 × 10⁻¹¹ s⁻¹ and T½ = 0.693/λ ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁰ s ≈ 470 years.
