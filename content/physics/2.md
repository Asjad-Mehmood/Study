Simple harmonic motion (S.H.M.) is to-and-fro motion where the acceleration always points back to the middle and grows with distance from it: a = −ω²x. Learn this one idea and the rest of the chapter follows from it.

**How to learn this chapter (about 3 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** learn the terms (amplitude, period, frequency, ω, phase) and the S.H.M. condition a = −ω²x. Then read the mass–spring and pendulum sections.
2. **Session 2:** practise the three derivations: spring, pendulum, and circle projection. Memory hook: "F = −kx, F = ma, so a = −(k/m)x".
3. **Session 3:** energy in S.H.M., damping and resonance. Then do the MCQs and numericals.
4. **Remember the extremes:** at the **mean** position speed and K.E. are maximum, while x, a and P.E. are zero. At the **extremes** it is the opposite.

### 1. Key ideas

**Oscillatory motion** is to-and-fro motion about a mean (equilibrium) position that repeats after equal time intervals. Examples: a pendulum, a mass on a spring, a tuning fork, a swing, atoms in a solid, guitar strings, a bird's wings.

**Basic terms**

- **Vibration (cycle):** one complete round trip, e.g. from A to B and back to A.
- **Displacement (x):** distance from the mean position at any instant.
- **Amplitude (x₀):** maximum displacement on either side. Larger amplitude means more energy.
- **Time period (T):** time for one vibration, in seconds.
- **Frequency (f):** vibrations per second, in hertz (Hz). f = 1/T.
- **Angular frequency (ω):** angle covered per second, in rad s⁻¹. ω = 2πf = 2π/T.

**What makes motion S.H.M.?** A **restoring force** that pulls back to the mean position and is proportional to displacement (Hooke's law, F = −kx). Then acceleration ∝ −x. The minus sign means acceleration always points towards the mean position. Not every vibration is S.H.M.; a heartbeat trace on an ECG is periodic but not S.H.M.

**Mass on a spring**: F = −kx and F = ma give a = −(k/m)x. So the motion is S.H.M. with ω = √(k/m). Its period T = 2π√(m/k) depends on mass and spring stiffness, not on amplitude.

**Simple pendulum**: a small heavy bob on a light, inextensible string. The component mg sinθ pulls the bob back; mg cosθ is cancelled by the string tension. For small angles (about 10° or less), sinθ ≈ θ (in radians), so a = −(g/ℓ)x. This is S.H.M. with T = 2π√(ℓ/g).

- T depends only on length ℓ and g. It does **not** depend on the bob's mass or (for small swings) the amplitude.
- A **second pendulum** has T = 2 s; its length is about 99.4 cm.
- A pendulum can be used to measure g: g = 4π²ℓ/T².

**S.H.M. and circular motion**: when a ball moves round a circle at steady speed, its shadow on a screen moves back and forth in S.H.M. So the projection of uniform circular motion on a diameter is S.H.M. This lets us find x, v and a easily.

**Phase**: the angle (ωt + φ) that tells exactly where the oscillator is and which way it is moving. φ is the phase constant (the starting phase). Two oscillators with a phase difference of 0° (or 360°) are **in phase**; with 180° they are **out of phase**.

**Graphs**: displacement, velocity and acceleration are all sine/cosine curves. Velocity is π/2 (a quarter cycle) ahead of displacement. Acceleration is exactly opposite to displacement (π apart).

**Energy in S.H.M.**: energy keeps switching between K.E. and P.E., but the total stays constant if there is no friction. Total energy = ½kx₀², so it is proportional to the **square of the amplitude**.

**Free oscillations**: the body vibrates at its own natural frequency with no outside force, and the amplitude stays constant (ideal case). Example: a pendulum disturbed once.

**Forced oscillations**: an outside periodic (driving) force keeps the body vibrating at the driver's frequency. Examples: a swing pushed repeatedly, a factory floor shaking from machines.

**Damped oscillations**: the amplitude gradually dies out because of friction or air resistance (damping forces). Types:

- **Light damping:** amplitude falls slowly; the body oscillates many times. Example: a playground swing.
- **Critical damping:** the body returns to rest in the shortest time without oscillating. Example: car shock absorbers.
- **Heavy damping:** the body returns very slowly without oscillating. Example: a pendulum in thick oil.

Car suspension uses (near) critical damping so the car settles quickly after a bump and passengers are comfortable.

**Resonance**: when the driving frequency equals the body's natural frequency (or a whole-number multiple of it), the amplitude becomes very large. The demonstration uses pendulums on a common rod: only the pendulums of the same length as the driver swing strongly.

**Sharpness of resonance**: the less the damping, the higher and narrower (sharper) the resonance peak. Heavy damping gives a low, flat curve. A lead bob responds much more than a pith ball of the same size, because air damps the light pith ball more.

| Resonance is useful | Resonance must be avoided |
| --- | --- |
| Microwave oven: microwaves (about 2.45 GHz, wavelength about 12 cm) make water molecules in food vibrate, producing heat | Bridges: soldiers break step so their marching does not match the bridge's natural frequency (Tacoma Narrows Bridge, 1940, collapsed in wind) |
| Radio tuning: the circuit's natural frequency is matched to the station's frequency | Aeroplane wings: engine or turbulence vibrations must not match the wing's natural frequency |
| Musical instruments: the sound box resonates and makes sound louder | Buildings in earthquakes: the 8 October 2005 Kashmir earthquake (magnitude 7.6) |
| MRI scans: radio waves make nuclei resonate |  |

**Standing waves and resonance (applications)**

- **Rubens tube:** a gas-filled metal pipe with holes on top and a speaker at one end. A standing sound wave makes flames tall where pressure is high and short where it is low, showing nodes and antinodes.
- **Chladni plate:** a metal plate vibrated at a resonant frequency. Sand on it collects at the nodes (still lines), making patterns.
- **Acoustic levitation:** ultrasound reflected between a transducer and a reflector forms a standing wave. Tiny objects (styrofoam bits, water drops) float, trapped at the nodes.

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Mass–spring system performs S.H.M.; its period and frequency**

1. Displace the mass m by x. The spring pulls back with F = −kx (Hooke's law).
2. Newton's second law: F = ma. So ma = −kx, giving a = −(k/m)x.
3. k/m is constant, so a ∝ −x. This is the condition for S.H.M.
4. Compare with a = −ω²x:

$$
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \qquad f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

**(b) Simple pendulum performs S.H.M.; its period**

1. Pull the bob aside by angle θ. Weight mg splits into mg cosθ (balanced by tension) and mg sinθ (the restoring force): F = −mg sinθ.
2. With F = ma: a = −g sinθ.
3. For small θ, sinθ ≈ θ, and θ = x/ℓ (arc ≈ straight displacement). So a = −(g/ℓ)x.
4. g/ℓ is constant, so a ∝ −x: the motion is S.H.M. Comparing with a = −ω²x:

$$
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}} \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}
$$

T depends on ℓ and g only, not on the mass of the bob or the amplitude.

**(c) Projection of uniform circular motion is S.H.M.; its velocity**

A particle P moves round a circle of radius x₀ with angular speed ω. Q is its projection on the diameter.

1. Displacement: after time t, angle θ = ωt, so x = x₀ cos ωt.
2. Velocity: P's speed is v₀ = x₀ω along the tangent. Q's velocity is its component along the diameter: v = x₀ω sinθ. Using sinθ = √(1 − cos²θ) and cosθ = x/x₀:

$$
v = x_0\omega\sqrt{1 - \frac{x^2}{x_0^2}} = \omega\sqrt{x_0^2 - x^2}
$$

3. At the mean position (x = 0), v is maximum = x₀ω. At the extremes (x = ±x₀), v = 0.
4. Acceleration: P's centripetal acceleration x₀ω² points to the centre; its component along the diameter is a = −x₀ω² cosθ = −ω²x. Since a ∝ −x, Q performs S.H.M.

**(d) Energy of a mass–spring system is conserved**

1. Force rises from 0 to kx₀ as the spring stretches, so the average force is ½kx₀. Work done = ½kx₀ × x₀ = ½kx₀². This is stored as P.E.; at any x, P.E. = ½kx².
2. Velocity at x is v = ω√(x₀² − x²), so:

$$
K.E. = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2(x_0^2 - x^2) = \tfrac{1}{2}k(x_0^2 - x^2) \quad (\text{since } k = m\omega^2)
$$

3. Add them:

$$
E = K.E. + P.E. = \tfrac{1}{2}k(x_0^2 - x^2) + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kx_0^2
$$

4. The total does not depend on x, so it is constant. At the mean position it is all K.E.; at the extremes it is all P.E.

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**14.1 Define S.H.M. and write its equation.** S.H.M. is vibratory motion in which acceleration is proportional to displacement from the mean position and always directed towards it. Equation: a = −ω²x (or a ∝ −x).

**14.2 Conditions for a frictionless system to execute S.H.M.** (i) There must be a restoring force proportional to displacement, F = −kx. (ii) The body must have inertia (mass) so it overshoots the mean position. (iii) Acceleration must be ∝ −x, always towards the mean position.

**14.3 Free vs forced oscillations.** Free: the body vibrates at its own natural frequency with no outside force after the first push (a pendulum released once). Forced: an external periodic force makes the body vibrate at the driver's frequency (a swing pushed regularly, a floor shaken by machines).

**14.4 Why can't we construct an ideal simple pendulum?** An ideal pendulum needs a point-mass bob, a massless and inextensible string, and no air resistance or friction at the support. Real bobs have size, real strings have mass and stretch, and air always causes damping.

**14.5 How does sharpness of resonance occur?** It depends on damping. With little damping, the amplitude rises steeply only very close to the natural frequency, so the peak is tall and narrow (sharp). With heavy damping, the peak is low and broad.

**14.6 What happens to the time period if Earth suddenly stops rotating?** Earth's rotation slightly reduces the effective g (except at the poles). If rotation stops, g increases a little, so a pendulum's period T = 2π√(ℓ/g) **decreases slightly** (no change at the poles). A spring's period would not change, since it does not depend on g.

**14.7 What is a restoring force? What provides it?** A force that always acts towards the mean position and is proportional to displacement. (i) Simple pendulum: the component of weight mg sinθ. (ii) Spring: the elastic force of the spring, F = −kx.

**14.8 Role of shock absorbers on a bumpy road.** They provide (near) critical damping. After each bump the car body returns to its normal position quickly without bouncing up and down, giving a smooth, safe ride and better tyre grip.

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**14.1 Why do marching troops break step on a bridge?** Regular steps form a periodic force. If its frequency matches the bridge's natural frequency, resonance occurs and the bridge may vibrate with a dangerously large amplitude and even collapse. Breaking step makes the force irregular, so resonance cannot build up.

**14.2 A girl swinging seated stands up. How does the period change?** Standing raises her centre of mass, so the effective length ℓ of the swing (pendulum) becomes shorter. Since T ∝ √ℓ, the period **decreases** and she swings faster.

**14.3 Will a pendulum correct at Karachi be accurate at Murree or on Everest?** No. g is smaller at greater height. Since T = 2π√(ℓ/g), T increases, so the clock runs **slow** (loses time) at Murree, and even more on top of Everest.

**14.4 How can we find the height of a tower using a wire hanging from it?** Tie a small heavy bob to the lower end to make a simple pendulum. Measure its period T with a stopwatch (time 20 vibrations and divide). Then ℓ = gT²/4π² gives the length of the wire, which is about the height of the tower.

**14.5 Adding more mass to a vibrating spring: effect on period?** T = 2π√(m/k), so the period **increases** (as √m). For example, four times the mass gives twice the period.

**14.6 Can a simple pendulum vibrate at the centre of the Earth?** No. At the centre, g = 0, so there is no restoring force (no weight). T = 2π√(ℓ/g) becomes infinite: the pendulum does not oscillate.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**14.1 Maximum acceleration and velocity.** x₀ = 0.05 m, f = 10 Hz, so ω = 2π(10) = 62.8 rad s⁻¹.

$$
a_{max} = \omega^2 x_0 = (62.8)^2(0.05) = 197\ \text{m s}^{-2} \qquad v_{max} = \omega x_0 = (62.8)(0.05) = 3.14\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**14.2 Period and frequency of a loaded spring.** m = 0.5 kg, extension 0.098 m. First find k from the weight: k = mg/x = (0.5 × 9.8)/0.098 = 50 N m⁻¹.

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.5}{50}} = 0.628\ \text{s} \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} = 1.59\ \text{Hz}
$$

**14.3 Energy stored and maximum speed.** F = 45 N, x = 0.10 m, so k = F/x = 450 N m⁻¹.

$$
E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(450)(0.10)^2 = 2.25\ \text{J} \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{max}^2 = 2.25 \Rightarrow v_{max} = \sqrt{\frac{2(2.25)}{0.5}} = 3\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**14.4 Body in S.H.M.** The book prints 80 kg, but its answers only work for **m = 8 kg**, so that is used here. x₀ = 0.30 m, F = 60 N at x = 0.30 m, so k = 60/0.30 = 200 N m⁻¹ and ω = √(200/8) = 5 rad s⁻¹.

- Period: T = 2π/ω = 2π/5 = **1.26 s ≈ 1.3 s**
- At x = 0.12 m, acceleration: a = ω²x = 25 × 0.12 = **3.0 m s⁻²**
- Speed: v = ω√(x₀² − x²) = 5√(0.09 − 0.0144) = 5 × 0.275 = **1.4 m s⁻¹**
- K.E. = ½mv² = ½(8)(1.375)² = **7.6 J**
- P.E. = ½kx² = ½(200)(0.12)² = **1.44 J** (check: total = ½kx₀² = 9 J = 7.56 + 1.44)

**14.5 Pendulum with T = 1 s.**

$$
\ell = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{9.8 \times 1^2}{4(3.14)^2} = 0.25\ \text{m} \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} = 1\ \text{Hz}
$$

**14.6 Block dropped onto a spring.** m = 5 kg, h = 0.8 m, k = 1960 N m⁻¹. The book's method: the block's loss of P.E. is stored in the spring.

$$
mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 \Rightarrow x = \sqrt{\frac{2mgh}{k}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(5)(9.8)(0.8)}{1960}} = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.2\ \text{m}
$$

Note: this ignores the extra fall of x while the spring compresses. Including it, mg(h + x) = ½kx² gives x ≈ 0.23 m. Write 0.2 m in the exam, since that is the book's answer.
