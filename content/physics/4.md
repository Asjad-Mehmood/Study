This chapter covers electric potential (voltage at a point), potential energy of charges, and capacitors: how they store charge and energy, how they combine, and how they discharge through a resistor. Core results: V = kq/r, C = ε₀εᵣA/d, energy = ½CV², and Q = Q₀e^(−t/RC).

**How to learn this chapter (about 3–4 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** potential and potential difference, E = −ΔV/Δr, V = kq/r, potential energy of two charges. Memory hook: "positive charges roll downhill in potential".
2. **Session 2:** capacitance, the parallel-plate formula, dielectrics. Derive C = ε₀A/d once from V = Ed and E = σ/ε₀.
3. **Session 3:** series and parallel combinations (note they are the opposite of resistors), and energy stored.
4. **Session 4:** RC discharge, time constant, 0.37 and 0.69 RC, uses of capacitors. Then all MCQs and the 9 numericals.

### 1. Key ideas

**Electrostatics** studies charges at rest: the forces between them (Coulomb's law, F = kq₁q₂/r², k = 1/4πε₀ = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²), the fields and potentials they make, and the energy they store.

**Potential difference (ΔV)**: the work done per unit charge in moving a charge between two points. ΔV = W/q. Unit: volt (V) = joule per coulomb.

**Field and potential**: the electric field equals the negative potential gradient, E = −ΔV/Δr. The minus sign means the field points from high potential to low potential. Between parallel plates (uniform field), E = V/d, so its unit can be written as V m⁻¹ (same as N C⁻¹).

**Electric potential (V) at a point**: the work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point, keeping it in equilibrium (moving it slowly). Infinity is taken as zero potential. Potential and potential difference are **scalars**.

- Due to a point charge: V = kq/r. It is positive near a positive charge and negative near a negative charge.
- Several charges: add the potentials algebraically (with signs). No vector addition is needed, which makes potential easier than field.

**Electric potential energy**: a positive charge, free to move, goes from high to low potential, losing P.E. and gaining K.E.: qΔV = ½mv². A negative charge moves from low to high potential.

- **Electron-volt (eV)**: the energy an electron gains when accelerated through 1 V. 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. It is a unit of energy, not potential.
- **P.E. of two point charges**: U = kQq/r. It equals the work done in bringing the charges from infinity to separation r. Like charges give positive U; unlike charges give negative U. U ∝ 1/r.

**Capacitor**: a device that stores charge and energy in the electric field between two conductors separated by an insulator (dielectric).

**Capacitance (C)**: C = Q/V, the charge stored per volt. Unit: farad (F) = C V⁻¹. One farad is huge, so we use μF, nF and pF.

- **Isolated sphere**: C = 4πε₀R (in a medium, 4πε₀εᵣR). A bigger sphere has a bigger C. C does not depend on Q or V.
- **Parallel-plate capacitor**: C = ε₀A/d in air. With a dielectric, C = ε₀εᵣA/d. Bigger plates, smaller gap and a better dielectric all increase C.

**Dielectric constant (relative permittivity, εᵣ)**: εᵣ = C\_with dielectric / C\_vacuum. It has no unit. For air, εᵣ ≈ 1.

**Why a dielectric increases C (polarization)**: a dielectric has no free electrons. In the field, each atom's electron cloud shifts slightly opposite to the field (the atom is polarized). Charges appear on the dielectric's surfaces, opposite to those on the nearby plates. This weakens E and lowers V for the same Q, so C = Q/V increases. A voltmeter across a charged capacitor shows V drop when a dielectric is slid in.

**Combinations of capacitors** (the reverse of resistors)

|  | Parallel | Series |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same for each capacitor | Voltage V | Charge Q |
| Shared between them | Charge: Q = Q₁ + Q₂ + Q₃ | Voltage: V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ |
| Equivalent capacitance | C\_e = C₁ + C₂ + C₃ (bigger than any one) | 1/C\_e = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + 1/C₃ (smaller than the smallest) |
| Use | To get a large capacitance | To share a high voltage safely |

**Energy stored in a capacitor**: work is needed to push extra charge onto plates that already repel it. On a V–Q graph (a straight line through the origin), the area under the line is the work done = ½QV. This energy is stored in the electric field between the plates. Energy per unit volume (energy density) = ½ε₀εᵣE².

**Charging and discharging (RC circuit)**

- Charging: the charge does not appear at once. It rises quickly at first, then slowly, towards the final value q₀ = CV₀.
- Discharging: charge, voltage and current all fall **exponentially**: x = x₀e^(−t/RC). Fast at first, then slower, like water draining from a tank through a narrow pipe.
- **Time constant τ = RC** (unit: ohm × farad = second). It is the time in which a charging capacitor reaches 63% of its final charge, or a discharging capacitor falls to 37% of its starting charge. A smaller RC means faster charging and discharging.
- **Half-time**: the charge halves in t½ = 0.69 RC.

**Uses of capacitors**

- **Fans and motors** (washing machines, blenders, water pumps): give an extra push at start-up to overcome static friction and inertia.
- **Refrigerators and air conditioners**: a large "start capacitor" jump-starts the compressor motor, then a relay disconnects it. A "run capacitor" stays in to improve efficiency. RC circuits also give a time delay that protects the compressor from power fluctuations.
- **Camera flashgun**: the capacitor slowly charges from the battery, then dumps its charge very quickly through a xenon flash tube, ionizing the gas and producing a bright flash.
- **Rectifiers**: smooth (filter) the output when converting AC to DC.
- **Medical**: defibrillators store the energy for a life-saving shock to the heart; capacitors filter noise in CT, MRI, X-ray, ultrasound, ECG and EEG machines.

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Absolute potential at a point due to a point charge: V = kq/r**

1. The field of q is not uniform (E ∝ 1/r²), so we cannot use V = Ed over a long distance. Instead take two points A and B very close together, at distances r\_A and r\_B from q.
2. Over this tiny gap, E is almost constant. Its value at the middle, distance r, is E = kq/r². Since A and B are very close, take r² ≈ r\_A r\_B. So E = kq/(r\_A r\_B).
3. Work done in moving a unit positive charge from B to A against the field = potential difference:

$$
V_A - V_B = E\,(r_B - r_A) = \frac{kq\,(r_B - r_A)}{r_A r_B} = kq\left(\frac{1}{r_A} - \frac{1}{r_B}\right)
$$

4. A long distance can be split into many such small steps, and the result adds up the same way. Now let B be at infinity, where V\_B = 0 and 1/r\_B = 0:

$$
V_A = \frac{kq}{r_A} \qquad\text{in general}\qquad V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r}
$$

**(b) Capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor: C = ε₀εᵣA/d**

1. Two plates, each of area A, a small distance d apart, with charges +Q and −Q. The field between them is uniform.
2. Surface charge density σ = Q/A. From Gauss's law, the field between the plates is E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A).
3. For a uniform field, V = Ed = Qd/(ε₀A).
4. Therefore:

$$
C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} \qquad\text{with a dielectric}\qquad C' = \frac{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r A}{d}
$$

5. Dividing the two gives εᵣ = C′/C, the definition of dielectric constant.

**(c) Combinations of capacitors**

Parallel: every capacitor has the same V; the total charge is the sum.

$$
Q = Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3 \Rightarrow C_eV = C_1V + C_2V + C_3V \Rightarrow C_e = C_1 + C_2 + C_3
$$

Series: the battery puts +Q on the first plate; each plate induces an equal and opposite charge on the next, so every capacitor carries the same Q. The voltages add up.

$$
V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3 \Rightarrow \frac{Q}{C_e} = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2} + \frac{Q}{C_3} \Rightarrow \frac{1}{C_e} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3}
$$

**(d) Energy stored from the area under the V–Q graph**

1. Since Q ∝ V, the graph of V against Q is a straight line from the origin to the final point (Q, V).
2. Adding a small charge ΔQ at voltage V needs work ΔW = VΔQ, the area of a thin strip under the line.
3. Adding all the strips from 0 to Q gives the area of the triangle: ½ × base × height.

$$
W = \tfrac{1}{2}QV = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}
$$

4. In terms of the field: put V = Ed and C = ε₀εᵣA/d to get W = ½ε₀εᵣE² × (Ad). Since Ad is the volume between the plates, energy per unit volume = ½ε₀εᵣE².

**(e) Time constant and half-time from the discharge equation**

1. Discharge: Q = Q₀e^(−t/RC). Put t = RC: Q = Q₀e⁻¹ = Q₀/2.718 = 0.37Q₀. So in one time constant the charge drops to 37%.
2. For the half-time, put Q = ½Q₀: ½ = e^(−t½/RC). Taking natural logs, ln(½) = −t½/RC, and ln(½) = −0.69:

$$
t_{1/2} = 0.69\,RC
$$

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**16.1 If the absolute potential at a point is zero, what about the field there?** The field need not be zero. E depends on how fast V changes (E = −ΔV/Δr), not on V itself. Example: midway between +q and −q, the potentials cancel (V = 0), but the field is strong, pointing towards −q.

**16.2 Is the electron-volt a unit of potential difference or energy?** Energy. It is the energy gained by an electron accelerated through 1 volt: 1 eV = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)(1 V) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**16.3 Define capacitance and give its units.** Capacitance is the charge stored per unit potential difference: C = Q/V. Unit: farad (F) = coulomb per volt. Smaller units: μF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).

**16.4 Define dielectric constant and give its formula.** The ratio of a capacitor's capacitance with the dielectric filling the gap to its capacitance with vacuum (air): εᵣ = C\_med/C\_vac. It has no unit and is always greater than 1 for a real dielectric.

**16.5 Effect of increasing plate separation.** C = ε₀εᵣA/d, so C ∝ 1/d. Increasing the separation **decreases** the capacitance.

**16.6 When is the energy stored in three capacitors greater: series or parallel?** With the same battery voltage, W = ½C\_eV². Parallel gives the largest C\_e (the sum) and series the smallest. So more energy is stored in **parallel**.

**16.7 What is the time constant?** τ = RC, the product of resistance and capacitance, measured in seconds. It is the time in which a discharging capacitor's charge falls to 37% of its initial value (or a charging capacitor reaches 63% of its final charge).

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**16.1 Potential vs potential energy, with an example.** Potential is a property of a point in the field: the energy per unit charge (J C⁻¹). It does not depend on what charge you place there. Potential energy belongs to a charge placed at that point: U = qV (J). Example: at a point where V = 100 V, a 1 C charge has U = 100 J, while a 2 C charge has U = 200 J. The potential is the same; the potential energy differs.

**16.2 How does potential explain the motion of charges?** Free positive charges move from high to low potential, like a ball rolling downhill, losing P.E. and gaining K.E. (qΔV = ½mv²). Negative charges (electrons) move from low to high potential. This is why current flows from the + terminal to the − terminal in the external circuit, while electrons flow the other way.

**16.3 Meaning of the negative sign in E = −ΔV/Δr, with an example.** It shows that the field points in the direction in which potential **decreases**. Example: plates at 6 V and 0 V. The field points from the 6 V plate to the 0 V plate, and the potential falls along that direction. A positive charge left between them moves the same way.

**16.4 No charge crosses the gap while charging, yet current flows. Explain.** Electrons are pulled off one plate and pushed onto the other plate through the wires and the battery. So current really flows in the external circuit. Inside the gap, no charge crosses, but the electric field between the plates keeps changing as charge builds up; this changing field completes the circuit (it is called displacement current).

**16.5 Energy stored in parallel vs series.** With the same voltage V, W = ½C\_eV². For two equal capacitors C: in series C\_e = C/2, so W = ¼CV²; in parallel C\_e = 2C, so W = CV². The parallel arrangement stores **four times** as much energy.

**16.6 Where is the energy stored: on the plates, in the dielectric, or in the field?** In the **electric field** between the plates (in the space filled by the dielectric). Work done in separating charge onto the plates creates the field, and the energy is spread through its volume with density ½ε₀εᵣE². The plates only hold the charges that produce the field.

**16.7 Why does a dielectric increase capacitance?** The dielectric's atoms become polarized. Induced charges on its surfaces oppose the charges on the nearby plates, reducing the field and potential difference between the plates for the same Q. Since C = Q/V and V falls, C rises, by the factor εᵣ.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**16.1 K.E. gained by a charge in a uniform field.** q = 0.1 μC, E = 60 N C⁻¹, d = 2 m. Work done by the field becomes K.E.:

$$
K.E. = qEd = (0.1\times10^{-6})(60)(2) = 1.2\times10^{-5}\ \text{J}
$$

**16.2 Voltage needed to give an electron 2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.**

$$
eV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \Rightarrow V = \frac{mv^2}{2e} = \frac{(9.11\times10^{-31})(2\times10^{6})^2}{2(1.6\times10^{-19})} = 11.4\ \text{V}
$$

**16.3 Work to move 40 μC from C to A.** V\_A − V\_B = 84 V and V\_C − V\_B = 24 V. Subtract: V\_A − V\_C = 84 − 24 = 60 V.

$$
W = q(V_A - V_C) = (40\times10^{-6})(60) = 2.4\times10^{-3}\ \text{J}
$$

**16.4 Dielectric constant.** C = 6 μF, A = 1.2 m², d = 9 × 10⁻⁶ m.

$$
\varepsilon_r = \frac{Cd}{\varepsilon_0 A} = \frac{(6\times10^{-6})(9\times10^{-6})}{(8.85\times10^{-12})(1.2)} = 5.1
$$

**16.5 Energy in a 15 μF capacitor at 300 V.**

$$
W = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(15\times10^{-6})(300)^2 = 0.675\ \text{J}
$$

**16.6 2.0 μF and 8.0 μF in series across 300 V.**

$$
C_e = \frac{C_1C_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{(2)(8)}{2 + 8} = 1.6\ \mu\text{F} \qquad Q = C_eV = (1.6\times10^{-6})(300) = 4.8\times10^{-4}\ \text{C}
$$

Same charge on each (series): V₁ = Q/C₁ = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ / 2 × 10⁻⁶ = **240 V**, and V₂ = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ / 8 × 10⁻⁶ = **60 V**. Check: 240 + 60 = 300 V.

**16.7 Number of electrons moved.** C = 3 μF, V = 240 V.

$$
Q = CV = (3\times10^{-6})(240) = 7.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{C} \qquad n = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{7.2\times10^{-4}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 4.5\times10^{15}
$$

The book prints 3.0 × 10¹⁵, which would need C = 2 μF. With the given 3 μF, the answer is 4.5 × 10¹⁵; check with your teacher.

**16.8 New separation when P.E. doubles.** U = kQq/r, so U ∝ 1/r. To double U, halve r: r = 1.2/2 = **0.6 m**.

**16.9 Voltage after 0.5 s of discharge.** C = 100 μF, R = 500 Ω, V₀ = 8 V. Time constant τ = RC = 500 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.05 s, so t = 0.5 s is 10 time constants.

$$
V = V_0e^{-t/RC} = 8\,e^{-0.5/0.05} = 8\,e^{-10} = 8(4.54\times10^{-5}) = 3.6\times10^{-4}\ \text{V}
$$
