This chapter links what we can measure in a gas (pressure, volume, temperature) to what its molecules are doing (mass, speed). The two results you must know by heart are PV = ⅓Nm⟨v²⟩ and ⟨K.E.⟩ = 3/2 kT.

**How to learn this chapter (about 3 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** read Key ideas once. Then close the notes and say the 5 assumptions of kinetic theory out loud.
2. **Session 2:** write derivation (a) on rough paper 2 times without looking, then derivation (b) once. Memory hook: "hit, bounce, 2L, add all, divide by 3".
3. **Session 3:** do the MCQs, then solve all 6 numericals yourself before checking the answers here.
4. **Revision:** the formula sheet and the two ratio rules (v ∝ √T, v ∝ 1/√M) solve most MCQs.

### 1. Key ideas

**Thermal physics** studies heat, work, temperature and energy. A system with a huge number of particles (about Avogadro's number) is **macroscopic**; it has temperature and pressure. A single atom or molecule is **microscopic**; it has kinetic energy and momentum.

**Brownian motion** is the random, zig-zag motion of tiny particles caused by molecules hitting them from all sides. Robert Brown saw pollen grains jiggling in water under a microscope. Einstein explained it in 1905, and Jean Perrin confirmed it in 1908 (Nobel Prize 1926).

- Lighter particles move faster, because a collision gives them more speed.
- Particles move faster in a less viscous (thinner) fluid. Viscosity is a fluid's internal friction.
- Brownian motion never stops, which is direct evidence that molecules exist and move.

**Kinetic theory of gases: the five assumptions of an ideal gas**

1. A gas has a very large number of tiny particles that are far apart compared with their size. Their own volume is negligible.
2. The particles move randomly and rapidly in all directions.
3. Collisions with each other and with the walls are perfectly elastic (no K.E. is lost).
4. There are no forces between particles except during collisions.
5. The average K.E. of the particles depends only on the temperature.

**Mean free path**: the average distance a molecule travels between two collisions.

**Where pressure comes from**: molecules hit the walls and bounce back. Each hit changes the molecule's momentum, so the wall feels a force. Pressure is this force per unit area. Heating makes molecules faster, so they hit harder and more often, and pressure rises.

**Ideal gas law**: PV = nRT = NkT, where n = N/N\_A, R = N\_A·k. Real gases behave nearly ideally at **high temperature and low pressure**, because the molecules are then far apart and fast, so attractions hardly matter.

**Why rms speed?** Molecules move in both directions, so the plain average velocity is zero. Squaring the speeds first, averaging, then taking the square root gives a useful "typical speed": the root mean square (rms) speed.

**Temperature and K.E.**: the average translational K.E. of a molecule is 3/2 kT. So temperature is a direct measure of molecular K.E. All gases at the same temperature have the same average K.E. per molecule, whatever their mass. Lighter molecules simply move faster.

At room temperature (300 K), kT ≈ 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J ≈ 0.026 eV. (1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; k = 8.62 × 10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹.)

**Daily-life examples**: popcorn pops because heated moisture inside the kernel turns to steam and the pressure bursts the shell. Pumping a tyre adds molecules, so more collisions per second with the walls, so higher pressure, so a harder tyre.

**Statistical physics and the Boltzmann law**: with billions of molecules we use probability. Molecules sit in different energy levels E₁, E₂, …. The Boltzmann distribution law says the number in a higher level falls exponentially with the energy gap: N₂/N₁ = e^(−ΔE/kT). So more molecules are always in the lower energy states. The term e^(−ΔE/kT) is the **Boltzmann factor**. Maxwell (1860) gave the distribution of molecular speeds, and Boltzmann extended it.

**Stellar evolution (kinetic theory applied to stars)**

1. A star begins as a cloud of gas and dust (a **nebula** or molecular cloud).
2. Gravity pulls the cloud inward. It gets denser and hotter (K.E. ∝ T), forming a **protostar**.
3. When the core is hot enough, **nuclear fusion** starts (hydrogen → helium). The star is stable when outward gas pressure balances inward gravity: **hydrostatic equilibrium**.
4. When hydrogen in the core runs out, the star swells into a **red giant** (or red supergiant if massive). Stars of at least half the Sun's mass then fuse helium into carbon and oxygen; massive stars go on fusing up to iron.
5. The end depends on mass:
   - Low or medium mass (like the Sun): outer layers drift off as a **planetary nebula**; the core becomes a **white dwarf**.
   - About 10 times the Sun's mass or more: a **supernova** explosion; the iron core collapses into a **neutron star** or a **black hole**.
   - **Red dwarfs** burn fuel so slowly that none has finished its life yet; they will end as low-mass white dwarfs.

If gravity > internal pressure, the star **contracts** and its core heats up (it may collapse). If internal pressure > gravity, the star **expands** (it may become a red giant).

**Neutron stars** are the densest objects after black holes. Inside, matter acts like a degenerate gas. Young, fast-spinning neutron stars sending out beams of radiation are **pulsars**. A teaspoon of neutron-star matter has a mass of about 5.5 × 10¹² kg.

**The Sun** formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a solar nebula. Its stages: nebula → protostar → main sequence (now) → red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf. It sends energy to Earth mainly by radiation.

**Parsec**: a distance unit astronomers use, about 3.26 light-years (about 31 trillion km).

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Pressure of a gas: P = ⅓ρ⟨v²⟩ and PV = ⅓Nm⟨v²⟩**

Take a cube of side L (volume V = L³) holding N molecules, each of mass m. Start with one molecule moving with velocity component vₓ towards the right-hand wall.

1. It hits the wall and rebounds elastically with −vₓ. Change in its momentum:

$$
\Delta p = (-m v_x) - (m v_x) = -2 m v_x
$$

2. It must travel 2L (to the opposite wall and back) before hitting the same wall again, so the time between hits is:

$$
\Delta t = \frac{2L}{v_x}
$$

3. By Newton's second law the wall pushes the molecule with force Δp/Δt. By Newton's third law the molecule pushes the wall with an equal force in the opposite direction:

$$
F = \frac{2 m v_x}{2L / v_x} = \frac{m v_x^2}{L}
$$

4. Pressure from this one molecule on the wall of area L²:

$$
P_1 = \frac{F}{L^2} = \frac{m v_x^2}{L^3}
$$

5. Add up all N molecules, each with its own speed:

$$
P = \frac{m}{L^3}\left(v_{1x}^2 + v_{2x}^2 + \dots + v_{Nx}^2\right) = \frac{N m}{V}\langle v_x^2 \rangle
$$

6. Motion is random, so no direction is special: ⟨vₓ²⟩ = ⟨v\_y²⟩ = ⟨v\_z²⟩. Since ⟨v²⟩ = ⟨vₓ²⟩ + ⟨v\_y²⟩ + ⟨v\_z²⟩, we get ⟨vₓ²⟩ = ⅓⟨v²⟩. Therefore:

$$
P = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{N m}{V}\,\langle v^2 \rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad PV = \frac{1}{3} N m \langle v^2 \rangle
$$

7. Total mass Nm divided by V is the density ρ, so:

$$
P = \frac{1}{3}\rho \langle v^2 \rangle \qquad v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3P}{\rho}}
$$

**(b) Temperature is a measure of average translational K.E.: ⟨K.E.⟩ = 3/2 kT**

1. From kinetic theory, PV = ⅓Nm⟨v²⟩. Write it as PV = ⅔N(½m⟨v²⟩) = ⅔N⟨K.E.⟩.
2. From the gas law, PV = NkT.
3. Equate the two right-hand sides:

$$
\frac{2}{3} N \langle K.E. \rangle = N k T \quad\Rightarrow\quad \langle K.E. \rangle = \frac{1}{2} m \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{3}{2} k T
$$

4. Since 3/2 and k are constants, ⟨K.E.⟩ ∝ T. Temperature (in kelvin) directly measures the average translational K.E. of the molecules.
5. Rearranging gives the rms speed in terms of temperature:

$$
v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}
$$

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**13.1 Do all gases have the same kinetic energy at the same temperature?** Yes, the same **average translational K.E. per molecule**, because ⟨K.E.⟩ = 3/2 kT depends only on T. Example: at 27 °C, an O₂ molecule and an H₂ molecule both have ⟨K.E.⟩ = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J. The H₂ molecule moves 4 times faster because it is 16 times lighter.

**13.2 Will the mean K.E. per molecule be equal in a mixture of two gases?** Yes. Both gases are at the same temperature (thermal equilibrium), and the mean K.E. per molecule depends only on temperature. Their speeds differ: the lighter gas moves faster.

**13.3 Density doubled, all else unchanged: effect on pressure?** From P = ⅓ρ⟨v²⟩, P ∝ ρ at the same ⟨v²⟩ (same temperature). So pressure **doubles**. More molecules per unit volume means more hits on the walls each second.

**13.4 A gas in a container is heated. What happens to the pressure?** Heating raises the molecules' average K.E., so they move faster. They hit the walls more often and with more momentum change each time, so the **pressure increases** (at constant volume, P ∝ T).

**13.5 Number of molecules doubled: effect on rms speed?** **No change**, if temperature stays the same. v\_rms = √(3kT/m) depends only on T and molecular mass, not on the number of molecules. (The pressure doubles, but not the speed.)

**13.6 Why can't we raise the temperature while keeping volume and pressure constant?** For a fixed amount of gas, PV = NkT, so T = PV/Nk. If P, V and N are fixed, T is fixed. Raising T would make molecules faster and raise P unless V grows. (Only by letting some gas escape, which reduces N, could T rise.)

**13.7 Reducing volume at constant temperature increases pressure. Explain.** Temperature is constant, so the molecules' speed stays the same. In a smaller volume, the molecules travel shorter distances between walls, so they hit each wall more often. More hits per second on a smaller area gives higher pressure. This is Boyle's law, P ∝ 1/V.

**13.8 How does the balance of gravity and internal pressure decide whether a star expands or contracts?** If gravity is greater, the star contracts, becoming denser and hotter in its core. If internal pressure is greater, the star expands (for example, into a red giant). When they are equal (hydrostatic equilibrium), the star is stable.

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**13.1 What will be the K.E. near absolute zero?** Since ⟨K.E.⟩ = 3/2 kT, as T → 0 K the average translational K.E. → 0. The molecules almost stop their random motion. (Quantum theory says a tiny "zero-point" energy remains, but classical kinetic theory gives zero.) This is why 0 K cannot actually be reached.

**13.2 Average K.E. of O₂ and N₂ molecules in a room (T ≈ 300 K)?**

$$
\langle K.E. \rangle = \tfrac{3}{2} k T = \tfrac{3}{2}(1.38\times10^{-23})(300) = 6.21\times10^{-21}\ \text{J} \approx 0.039\ \text{eV}
$$

It is the **same for both**. Their rms speeds differ: about 483 m s⁻¹ for O₂ and 517 m s⁻¹ for N₂ at 300 K.

**13.3 Factor by which temperature changes in a sealed container (fixed N):** T ∝ PV.

- (a) V doubled and P doubled: T becomes 2 × 2 = **4 times**.
- (b) V halved and P tripled: T becomes ½ × 3 = **1.5 times**.

**13.4 Can water boil at room temperature without heating?** Yes. Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure above it. If we lower the pressure above the water with a vacuum pump, its boiling point drops. At about 3.2 kPa, water boils at around 25 °C.

**13.5 How does a drop of ink behave in a beaker of water?** The ink slowly spreads through the whole beaker without stirring. Water molecules, in constant random motion, keep hitting the ink particles from all sides (Brownian motion), so the ink diffuses from high concentration to low until the colour is even. It spreads faster in hot water because the molecules move faster.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**13.1 rms velocity of hydrogen at STP.** Given ρ = 8.957 × 10⁻² kg m⁻³; STP pressure = 76 cm of mercury.

$$
P = h\rho_{Hg} g = 0.76 \times 13600 \times 9.8 = 1.013\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}
$$

$$
v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3P}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\times1.013\times10^{5}}{8.957\times10^{-2}}} = \sqrt{3.39\times10^{6}} = 1.84\times10^{3}\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**13.2 Pressure of oxygen at 0 °C.** Given ρ = 1.44 kg m⁻³, v\_rms = 456.4 m s⁻¹.

$$
P = \frac{1}{3}\rho v_{rms}^2 = \frac{1}{3}(1.44)(456.4)^2 = 1.0\times10^{5}\ \text{N m}^{-2}
$$

**13.3 Temperature at which v\_rms is three times its STP value.** v\_rms ∝ √T, so T must be 3² = 9 times larger.

$$
T_2 = 9 \times 273\ \text{K} = 2457\ \text{K} = 2457 - 273 = 2184\ ^{\circ}\text{C}
$$

**13.4 Number of molecules per m³.** Given T = 80 °C = 353 K, P = 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ N m⁻², V = 1 m³.

$$
\frac{N}{V} = \frac{P}{kT} = \frac{5\times10^{-10}}{(1.38\times10^{-23})(353)} = 1.02\times10^{11}\ \text{molecules per m}^3
$$

**13.5 rms speed of free electrons in the Sun's atmosphere.** Given T = 2.00 × 10⁶ K, m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg. (The pressure is not needed.)

$$
v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.38\times10^{-23})(2.00\times10^{6})}{9.11\times10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{9.09\times10^{13}} = 9.5\times10^{6}\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**13.6 Gravitational force inside a star.** Given M\_r = 2.0 × 10³⁰ kg, r = 7.0 × 10⁸ m, ρ = 1500 kg m⁻³. Force on a mass m at radius r:

$$
F = \frac{G M_r m}{r^2} = \frac{(6.67\times10^{-11})(2.0\times10^{30})}{(7.0\times10^{8})^2}\, m = 2.72\times10^{2}\, m\ \text{N}
$$

So the force is about 2.7 × 10² N on each kilogram. The book prints 2.94 × 10² N; working the same numbers carefully gives 2.72 × 10², so check this with your teacher. Using the density, the pressure gradient is |ΔP/Δr| = GM\_rρ/r² = 272 × 1500 ≈ 4.1 × 10⁵ Pa per metre.

**Book examples to practise** (same method): a star with M = 2 × 10³⁰ kg and R = 7 × 10⁸ m has P\_g ≈ GM²/R⁴ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵ Pa, about equal to its internal pressure, so it is stable. A star where P\_g is larger contracts; one where internal pressure is larger expands.
