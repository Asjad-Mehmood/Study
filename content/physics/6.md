At atomic scale, light comes in packets (photons) and particles behave like waves. This chapter shows the evidence: the photoelectric effect and Compton effect (light as particles), electron diffraction (particles as waves), line spectra (energy levels), and the uncertainty principle.

**How to learn this chapter (about 3 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** photons (E = hf, p = h/λ) and the photoelectric effect. Learn the 3 observations and why wave theory fails. Memory hook: "frequency decides energy, intensity decides number".
2. **Session 2:** Compton effect, pair production and annihilation, de Broglie waves, Davisson–Germer, electron microscope.
3. **Session 3:** spectra (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen) and uncertainty. Then MCQs and the 10 numericals; keep h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J on your rough sheet.

### 1. Key ideas

**Quantum theory of radiation (Planck, 1901)**: energy is emitted or absorbed in small separate packets called **quanta** (singular: quantum), not continuously. Each quantum has energy E = hf, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s (Planck's constant). Planck won the Nobel Prize in 1918.

**Photon (Einstein)**: light and all electromagnetic radiation consist of indivisible packets of energy called photons, moving at speed c. Each photon has energy E = hf = hc/λ and momentum p = E/c = h/λ, even though its rest mass is zero. Because photons carry momentum, light can exert a force (radiation pressure). Quantum effects matter only for atom-sized objects; for everyday objects the energy steps are far too small to notice.

**Photoelectric effect**: electrons are emitted from a metal surface when light of suitable frequency falls on it. The emitted electrons are **photoelectrons**.

- Apparatus: an evacuated tube with a metal cathode C and an anode. Light on C releases electrons, which the positive anode collects, so a micro-ammeter shows current.
- Reverse the battery (anode negative) and raise the voltage: the current falls and becomes zero at the **stopping potential V₀**. Then K.E.\_max = eV₀.

**Observations**

1. There is a minimum **threshold frequency f₀** (or maximum threshold wavelength λ₀) below which no electrons come out, however bright the light. Blue light ejects electrons from sodium; red light does not.
2. Above f₀, K.E.\_max increases with frequency. It does **not** depend on intensity.
3. Brighter light ejects **more** electrons (more current) but with the same K.E.\_max (same V₀).
4. Emission is instantaneous; there is no delay.

Wave theory fails: it predicts that brighter light should give faster electrons, that any frequency should work if we wait long enough, and that there should be a delay. None of this happens.

**Einstein's explanation (Nobel Prize 1921)**: one photon gives all its energy hf to one electron. The electron uses part of it, the **work function φ**, to escape the metal; the rest is its kinetic energy: hf = φ + ½mv²\_max. If hf < φ, no electron escapes, however many photons arrive. Brighter light means more photons, so more electrons, but each photon still has the same energy. This proves the **particle nature** of light.

**Compton effect (1923)**: X-rays scattered by loosely bound electrons in graphite come out with a **longer wavelength**. Compton treated the X-ray photon and the electron like colliding billiard balls, conserving both energy and momentum. The shift is Δλ = (h/m₀c)(1 − cosθ). The quantity h/m₀c = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m is the **Compton wavelength**; at θ = 90°, the shift equals it. This is more proof of the particle nature of light (Nobel Prize 1927).

**Pair production and annihilation**: a high-energy γ-ray photon (at least 1.02 MeV) passing near a nucleus can turn into an electron and a positron: photon energy = 2m₀c² + K.E. of the pair. In **annihilation**, an electron and positron meet and vanish, producing two γ-ray photons: e⁺ + e⁻ → γ + γ.

**de Broglie waves (1924)**: if light (a wave) can act like particles, then particles should act like waves. A particle of momentum p = mv has a wavelength λ = h/mv. For everyday objects λ is far too small to detect (a 20 g bullet at 330 m s⁻¹ has λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m). For electrons it is about the size of X-ray wavelengths, so electron diffraction can be observed. de Broglie won the Nobel Prize in 1929.

**Davisson–Germer experiment**: electrons accelerated through 54 V were fired at a nickel crystal. The scattered electrons came off in peaks, a diffraction pattern, just like X-rays. The measured wavelength (from Bragg's law, 1.65 × 10⁻¹⁰ m) matched de Broglie's prediction (1.66 × 10⁻¹⁰ m). This proves the **wave nature of particles**. Protons, neutrons and atoms also diffract.

**Wave–particle duality**: interference and diffraction show light is a wave; the photoelectric and Compton effects show it is particles. Electrons likewise show both. Bohr's **principle of complementarity**: both aspects are needed for a full description, but a single experiment shows only one. Light travels like a wave and interacts with matter like particles.

**Electron microscope**: uses the wave nature of electrons. Their wavelength is thousands of times shorter than visible light, so it can show far finer detail (down to nanometres, near atomic size). Magnetic lenses do the focusing: a condenser lens focuses the beam on the specimen, an objective lens forms the image, and a projector lens magnifies it onto a screen or film (a micrograph). Higher accelerating voltage gives faster electrons, shorter λ and better resolution. Scanning electron microscopes give 3-D images. Uses: viruses, bacteria, cell structure, metal fractures, crystal structure.

**Atomic spectra**: a hot gas of an element, viewed through a spectrometer with a grating, gives sharp bright lines at certain wavelengths: a **line spectrum**, unique to each element (like a fingerprint).

- Electrons in an atom can have only certain energies (discrete energy levels). n = 1 is the **ground state**; n = 2, 3, … are **excited states**. For hydrogen, Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV.
- An excited electron drops to a lower level and emits a photon with hf = E\_upper − E\_lower. Each drop gives one spectral line.
- **Hydrogen series**: Lyman (ends at n = 1, ultraviolet), Balmer (ends at n = 2, visible, discovered 1885), Paschen (ends at n = 3, infrared).
- **Emission spectrum**: bright lines on a dark background, given out by a hot gas.
- **Absorption spectrum**: white light passed through a cool gas shows dark lines where the gas absorbed exactly the photons needed to lift electrons up. The dark absorption lines sit at exactly the same wavelengths as that element's bright emission lines.
- A photon must have exactly the right energy to excite an atom, but a colliding electron with equal or more K.E. can excite it.

**Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927)**: we cannot measure both the position and momentum of a particle exactly at the same time: Δx Δp ≈ h. This is not a fault of instruments; it comes from wave–particle duality. To locate an electron precisely we need short-wavelength light, but such a photon has high momentum and knocks the electron, spoiling the momentum measurement. A similar form: ΔE Δt ≈ h. The effect is negligible for a tennis ball but huge for an electron. More careful forms use ħ = h/2π = 1.05 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e.g. Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2. Heisenberg won the Nobel Prize in 1932.

**Why no electrons inside a nucleus**: a nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁴ m across. Confining an electron there gives Δp ≈ h/Δx ≈ 6.6 × 10⁻²⁰ kg m s⁻¹, which means an energy of about a hundred MeV or more. No electron with such energy is ever found in atoms, so electrons do not live inside the nucleus.

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Photon momentum p = h/λ**: from relativity, E = mc² and the photon's momentum is p = mc, so pc = E = hf. Therefore p = hf/c, and since c = fλ, p = h/λ.

**(b) Einstein's photoelectric equation**

1. A photon of energy hf is absorbed by one electron in the metal.
2. The electron spends energy φ (the work function) to escape from the surface. The rest becomes its maximum K.E.:

$$
hf = \phi + \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{max}^2
$$

3. At the threshold frequency, the electron just escapes with zero K.E.: hf₀ = φ. Substituting:

$$
hf = hf_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{max}^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{max}^2 = eV_0
$$

4. Only electrons from the very surface get this maximum; deeper ones lose some energy in collisions.

**(c) Compton shift (outline)**: treat the collision as elastic. Energy: hc/λ − hc/λ′ = (m − m₀)c². Momentum along the original direction: h/λ = (h/λ′)cosθ + mv cosφ. Perpendicular: 0 = (h/λ′)sinθ − mv sinφ. Solving these three equations gives:

$$
\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0c}(1 - \cos\theta)
$$

**(d) Wavelength of an electron accelerated through V**: the gain in K.E. is ½mv² = eV, so mv = √(2meV), and:

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}
$$

For V = 54 V this gives λ = 1.66 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, matching the Davisson–Germer result.

**(e) Uncertainty relation (simple form)**: using light of wavelength λ to find a particle gives Δx ≈ λ. The photon can give up to all its momentum h/λ to the particle, so Δp ≈ h/λ. Multiplying: Δx Δp ≈ h.

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**18.1 How is the energy of a packet related to its frequency?** Each packet (quantum or photon) has energy directly proportional to its frequency: E = hf. Higher-frequency radiation (UV, X-rays) carries more energy per photon than low-frequency radiation (infrared, radio).

**18.2 How does photoelectric current vary with intensity?** Above the threshold frequency, current is directly proportional to intensity. Brighter light has more photons per second, each freeing one electron, so more electrons flow. The K.E. of each electron stays the same.

**18.3 Which has more energy: a UV photon or a yellow-light photon?** The UV photon, because UV has a higher frequency (shorter wavelength) and E = hf.

**18.4 Is the work function related to the threshold frequency?** Yes, directly: φ = hf₀. A metal with a larger work function needs a higher threshold frequency (a shorter threshold wavelength).

**18.5 Why can we see wave properties of electrons but not of a tennis ball?** λ = h/mv. An electron's tiny mass gives a wavelength about 10⁻¹⁰ m, comparable to atomic spacing in crystals, so diffraction shows. A tennis ball's large mass gives a wavelength around 10⁻³⁴ m, far too small for any slit or detector.

**18.6 The same radiation ejects electrons from one metal but not another. Why?** Different metals have different work functions. The photon energy hf is larger than the first metal's work function but smaller than the second's, so only the first emits electrons.

**18.7 Does high-frequency light give more electrons than low-frequency light?** Not necessarily. The number of electrons depends on the number of photons (intensity), not on frequency. Higher frequency gives each electron more K.E. (as long as f > f₀).

**18.8 When does light act as a wave and when as particles?** It acts as a wave while travelling and spreading: interference, diffraction, polarization. It acts as particles when it exchanges energy with matter: photoelectric effect, Compton effect, pair production.

**18.9 Name two spectral series of hydrogen and their regions.** Lyman series, in the ultraviolet; Balmer series, in the visible (Paschen, in the infrared, is a third).

**18.10 What is meant by the shift (Compton shift), and what does it depend on?** The increase in wavelength of X-rays after scattering from electrons: Δλ = (h/m₀c)(1 − cosθ). It depends only on the scattering angle θ (and the mass of the scattering particle), not on the incident wavelength or the target material.

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**18.1 Why do hot solids give a continuous spectrum but hot vapours a line spectrum?** In a solid, atoms are packed tightly and interact strongly, so their energy levels spread into broad bands; electrons can jump by almost any amount, giving all wavelengths. In a thin vapour, atoms are far apart and independent, so each keeps its sharp energy levels, and only certain jumps (wavelengths) are possible.

**18.2 Light can emit electrons from a metal and can also be diffracted. Comment.** This shows wave–particle duality. Photoelectric emission needs light to be photons (particles) delivering hf each; diffraction needs light to be a wave. Light has both natures; each experiment reveals one.

**18.3 Why do X-rays differ from light though both come from electron transitions?** Light comes from outer electrons jumping between closely spaced levels (a few eV). X-rays come from inner electrons in heavy atoms jumping between deep levels thousands of eV apart. So X-ray photons have much higher energy and shorter wavelength, which lets them penetrate matter.

**18.4 Is energy conserved when an atom emits a photon?** Yes. The atom's energy falls from E\_upper to E\_lower, and the photon carries away exactly the difference: hf = E\_upper − E\_lower. (The atom also recoils slightly, conserving momentum.)

**18.5 How can hydrogen, with one electron, give many spectral lines?** A hydrogen gas sample has billions of atoms. Different atoms are excited to different levels and drop back through different routes (e.g. 3→2, 4→2, 3→1). Each different jump gives a different line, so the sample shows the full set of lines at once.

**18.6 What speed would an electron need if confined to a nucleus-sized box?** Take Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m. Then Δp ≈ h/Δx = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 10⁻¹⁴ = 6.6 × 10⁻²⁰ kg m s⁻¹, and Δv = Δp/m = 6.6 × 10⁻²⁰ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ ≈ 7 × 10¹⁰ m s⁻¹. That is more than the speed of light, which is impossible, so electrons cannot be confined inside a nucleus. (A relativistic treatment gives energies over 100 MeV, never observed.)

**18.7 Scattered X-ray compared with the incident one.** Wavelength: longer. Frequency: lower. Energy: less (some was given to the electron). Speed: the same, c, because all electromagnetic waves travel at c.

**18.8 Why do energy and momentum conservation play a key role in the Compton effect?** Compton treated the photon–electron collision like two billiard balls. Applying conservation of energy and of momentum (in two directions) to this collision gives the formula for the shift exactly. The agreement with experiment proved photons carry momentum h/λ, i.e. behave as particles.

**18.9 How does a line spectrum show discrete energy levels?** Each element emits only a few sharp wavelengths. Since each photon's energy is hf = E\_upper − E\_lower, only certain energy differences can exist. That is possible only if electrons occupy fixed, separate energy levels, not any energy.

**18.10 A photon has zero mass, so how does it have momentum?** Its rest mass is zero, but it always moves at c and carries energy E = hf. In relativity, momentum is linked to energy: p = E/c = h/λ. Its momentum shows up in the Compton effect and in radiation pressure (e.g. solar sails).

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**18.1 Energy of a 350 nm photon.**

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{350\times10^{-9}} = 5.68\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} = \frac{5.68\times10^{-19}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 3.55\ \text{eV}
$$

**18.2 Frequency for an energy gap of 3 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.**

$$
f = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{3\times10^{-19}}{6.63\times10^{-34}} = 4.5\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}
$$

**18.3 Wavelength of a photon of energy 10 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.**

$$
\lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{1.0\times10^{-18}} = 1.99\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}\ (199\ \text{nm})
$$

**18.4 Threshold frequency of sodium (φ = 2.28 eV).**

$$
f_0 = \frac{\phi}{h} = \frac{2.28\times1.6\times10^{-19}}{6.63\times10^{-34}} = 5.5\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}
$$

**18.5 Longest wavelength for tungsten (φ = 4.54 eV).**

$$
\lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\phi} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{4.54\times1.6\times10^{-19}} = 2.73\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 273\ \text{nm}
$$

**18.6 Electron accelerated through 550 V.**

$$
p = \sqrt{2meV} = \sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.6\times10^{-19})(550)} = 1.27\times10^{-23}\ \text{N s} \qquad \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = 5.22\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}
$$

**18.7 Uncertainty in velocity for Δx = 10⁻⁷ m.** The book's answer uses the careful form Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2:

$$
\Delta v = \frac{\hbar}{2m\,\Delta x} = \frac{1.05\times10^{-34}}{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(10^{-7})} \approx 6\times10^{2}\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

After 1 s, the electron could be anywhere within about 6 × 10² m. (Using the simple form Δx Δp ≈ h instead gives Δv ≈ 7.3 × 10³ m s⁻¹; follow your teacher's choice.)

**18.8 Nickel nucleus emits a 1.17 MeV photon.** E = 1.17 × 10⁶ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.87 × 10⁻¹³ J.

$$
\lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = 1.06\times10^{-12}\ \text{m} \qquad p = \frac{E}{c} = 6.25\times10^{-22}\ \text{N s}
$$

Momentum is conserved: the nucleus recoils with the same momentum.

$$
v = \frac{p}{M} = \frac{6.25\times10^{-22}}{9.95\times10^{-26}} = 6.29\times10^{3}\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**18.9 Will 450 nm light eject electrons from a metal with φ = 3.5 eV?**

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{450\times10^{-9}} = 4.42\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 2.76\ \text{eV}
$$

2.76 eV < 3.5 eV, so **no electrons are emitted**.

**18.10 Compton scattering of a 90 keV X-ray.**

$$
\lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{90\times10^{3}\times1.6\times10^{-19}} = 1.38\times10^{-11}\ \text{m} = 13.8\ \text{pm}
$$

- (a) θ = 30°: Δλ = 2.43 × (1 − cos 30°) = 0.33 pm, so λ′ = 13.8 + 0.33 = **14.1 pm**
- (b) θ = 60°: Δλ = 2.43 × (1 − cos 60°) = 1.21 pm, so λ′ = 13.8 + 1.21 = **15.0 pm**
