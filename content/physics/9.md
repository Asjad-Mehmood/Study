The first half uses light from stars to find their power, distance, temperature and size, and shows that the universe is expanding (redshift, Hubble's law, Big Bang). The second half explains Earth's climate: the energy budget, winds, the Coriolis effect and ocean circulation. Five formulas carry the numericals: L = 4πr²σT⁴, F = L/4πd², λ\_max T = b, z = Δλ/λ₀ = v/c, and v = H₀d.

**How to learn this chapter (about 2–3 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** luminosity, inverse-square law, standard candles, blackbody radiation, Wien's law, Stefan–Boltzmann law, radius of a star.
2. **Session 2:** spectra and redshift, Hubble's law, the balloon model and Big Bang, satellite remote sensing.
3. **Session 3:** climate system (5 spheres), energy budget, equator–pole imbalance, Coriolis effect and the 3 cells, salinity, thermohaline circulation. Then MCQs and the 8 numericals.
4. **Memory hook:** "Hot is short": hotter stars peak at shorter wavelengths (blue); cooler ones at longer (red).

### 1. Key ideas

**Luminosity (L)**: the total power a star gives out as electromagnetic radiation, in watts. It is the star's true (intrinsic) brightness. It depends on the star's size (surface area) and surface temperature. The Sun's luminosity is about 4 × 10²⁶ W.

**Stefan–Boltzmann law**: L = 4πr²σT⁴, where σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. The total power of a star is proportional to its surface area and to the fourth power of its temperature. Doubling the temperature makes it radiate 2⁴ = **16 times** more.

**Radiant flux intensity (F)**: the power received per square metre at a distance d from the star: F = L/4πd². This is what we measure on Earth (apparent brightness). It follows the **inverse-square law**: at twice the distance F is ¼, at three times it is 1/9. So two stars can look equally bright even if one is far more luminous but farther away (10 times farther means 100 times more luminous).

**Standard candles**: objects whose true luminosity is known, such as Cepheid variable stars (Henrietta Swan Leavitt, 1910s: their pulsing period reveals their luminosity) and Type Ia supernovae. Measure F, know L, then find the distance from d = √(L/4πF). Without standard candles we could not measure the size of the universe.

**Blackbody**: an ideal object that absorbs all radiation falling on it and emits radiation of all wavelengths perfectly. A good real example is a hollow cavity, blackened inside with soot, with a small hole: radiation that enters gets trapped. The radiation it gives out (blackbody radiation) depends only on its temperature, not its material or shape.

- As temperature rises, a heated object glows dull red, then yellow, then white: its peak shifts to shorter wavelengths, and its total energy increases.
- Stars, which generate energy by fusion, behave like blackbodies and give a continuous spectrum. Humans emit blackbody radiation in the infrared (night-vision cameras detect it).

**Wien's displacement law**: the wavelength at which a blackbody emits most strongly is inversely proportional to its temperature: λ\_max T = b, with b = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K. Hot stars look blue-white; cool stars look red. Measuring λ\_max gives a star's surface temperature.

**Radius of a star**: (1) find T from Wien's law; (2) find L from the flux F measured on Earth and the distance (L = 4πd²F); (3) put L and T into the Stefan–Boltzmann law and solve for r.

**Star spectra**: the hot surface gives a continuous spectrum. As the light passes through the star's cooler outer gas, atoms absorb particular wavelengths, giving dark **absorption lines**. These tell us which elements the star contains.

**Redshift**: in light from distant galaxies, every spectral line appears at a **longer wavelength** than in the laboratory, shifted towards red. The redshift is z = Δλ/λ₀ ≈ v/c (for speeds much less than c).

- A positive Δλ (redshift) means the source is moving away; a negative Δλ (blueshift) means it is approaching.
- **Cosmic redshift**: space itself is stretching as the universe expands, and it stretches the light waves travelling through it. Redshift is seen in every direction, and farther galaxies show larger redshifts.

**Hubble's law**: the recession speed of a galaxy is directly proportional to its distance: v = H₀d. H₀ ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ (= 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹). On a graph of v against d, the straight line's slope is H₀.

**Balloon model and the Big Bang**: dots on a balloon being blown up all move apart, and every dot sees the others moving away; there is no centre. In the same way every galaxy sees the others receding, and Earth is not at the centre. Running time backwards, galaxies were closer in the past, and everything was once packed into an extremely hot, dense state. This is the **Big Bang theory**: the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since. Hubble's law is one of its main pieces of evidence.

**Satellite remote sensing** (studying Earth from orbit without touching it) has 6 steps:

1. **Energy source:** electromagnetic radiation lights up the target.
2. **Atmosphere:** the radiation is scattered or absorbed on the way.
3. **Interaction with the target:** the target reflects or emits radiation according to its properties.
4. **Recording by sensor:** sensors on the satellite record it.
5. **Transmission and reception:** data are sent to a ground station and turned into images.
6. **Interpretation and analysis:** information is extracted for applications (deforestation, climate change, ocean temperature, air quality).

**Earth's climate system**: climate is the long-term pattern of temperature, rainfall and wind. It has five interacting parts; a change in one affects the others:

| Component | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Atmosphere | Air |
| Hydrosphere | Water: oceans, rivers, lakes |
| Cryosphere | Ice, snow and permafrost (frozen ground) |
| Lithosphere | Earth's upper rocky layer (land) |
| Biosphere | All living things |

**Ocean currents**: surface currents are driven mainly by wind. Earth's rotation turns them into large circular systems called **gyres**, clockwise in the northern hemisphere and anticlockwise in the southern. They carry warm water from the equator towards the poles and cold water back, spreading heat around the planet.

**Wind patterns**: uneven heating makes warm air rise and cool air move in, forming wind belts: **trade winds** near the equator, **westerlies** in mid-latitudes, **polar easterlies** near the poles. Winds also push ocean currents.

**Earth's energy budget**: the balance between solar energy absorbed by Earth and energy radiated back to space as infrared. Earth absorbs about 70% of incoming sunlight; clouds, ice and deserts reflect the rest. When the budget balances, the average temperature stays steady.

- Ice–albedo feedback: a colder Earth has more ice, which reflects more sunlight, making it colder still. A warmer Earth melts ice, exposing dark land and sea that absorb more, making it warmer still.
- **Greenhouse gases** (CO₂, methane) trap outgoing infrared, so less energy escapes and Earth warms: **global warming**. Even a 1% imbalance can cause big temperature changes.

**Equator–pole energy imbalance**: the equator gets direct, intense sunlight; warm air rises there, making low pressure. The poles get slanting, weak sunlight; cold air sinks, making high pressure. This difference drives the global winds and currents that carry heat towards the poles.

**Coriolis effect and atmospheric cells**: air keeps its specific angular momentum (vr) as it moves north or south. Because Earth's surface moves faster at the equator than near the poles, moving air gets ahead of or lags behind the ground under it, so its path curves: **to the right in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern**. Its speed does not change, only its direction. These deflected flows form three circulation cells in each hemisphere: the **Hadley**, **Ferrel** and **Polar** cells.

**Salt, density and ocean circulation**: salinity is the amount of dissolved salt. Salt adds mass without much extra volume, so saltier water is denser; colder water is also denser. When surface water gets colder and saltier (from evaporation or when sea ice forms and leaves the salt behind), it sinks, pushing other water aside and starting slow, deep currents. These carry heat, oxygen and CO₂ around the globe. (The Dead Sea is so salty that people float easily.)

**Thermohaline circulation ("global conveyor belt")**: driven by differences in temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline). Warm, less dense surface water flows from the tropics towards the poles, carrying heat. Near the poles it cools, gets saltier as sea ice forms, becomes dense and sinks, then flows back towards the equator along the ocean floor before rising again elsewhere. One full cycle takes about 1000 years. It regulates climate and spreads heat and nutrients.

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Inverse-square law for flux**: a star sends out power L equally in all directions. At distance d this power is spread over a sphere of area 4πd², so the power per unit area is:

$$
F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad F \propto \frac{1}{d^2}
$$

**(b) Radius of a star from Wien's and Stefan–Boltzmann laws**

1. Wien's law gives the temperature: T = b/λ\_max.
2. The measured flux and known distance give the luminosity: L = 4πd²F.
3. Stefan–Boltzmann law, solved for r:

$$
L = 4\pi r^2\sigma T^4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}}
$$

**(c) Speed from redshift, then distance**: for speeds much smaller than light, z = Δλ/λ₀ ≈ v/c, so v = zc. Then Hubble's law gives d = v/H₀.

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**21.1 Define luminosity.** The total energy a star emits per second as electromagnetic radiation (its total power), in watts. It shows the star's true brightness.

**21.2 State the inverse-square law for radiant flux and its significance.** The flux received from a star is inversely proportional to the square of the distance: F = L/4πd². Significance: how bright a star looks depends on distance as well as luminosity, so a faint-looking star may be very powerful but far away. If we know L and measure F, we can find the distance.

**21.3 What is a standard candle and how is it used?** An astronomical object whose true luminosity is known, such as a Cepheid variable or a Type Ia supernova. Measure its apparent flux F on Earth, then use F = L/4πd² to calculate its distance, and hence the distance of its galaxy.

**21.4 State the Stefan–Boltzmann law and the quantities it relates.** The total power radiated by a star is proportional to its surface area and the fourth power of its absolute temperature: L = 4πr²σT⁴. It relates luminosity L, radius r and surface temperature T (σ is a constant).

**21.5 How can you estimate a star's radius using Wien's and Stefan–Boltzmann laws?** Find T from the peak wavelength (T = b/λ\_max). Find L from the measured flux and distance. Then r = √(L/4πσT⁴).

**21.6 State Hubble's law and its link to the Big Bang.** A galaxy's recession speed is proportional to its distance: v = H₀d. Since all galaxies are moving apart, they must have been closer together in the past; going back far enough, all matter was in one extremely hot, dense state. This supports the Big Bang theory.

**21.7 List the five components of Earth's climate system.** Atmosphere (air), hydrosphere (water), cryosphere (ice and permafrost), lithosphere (rocky land), biosphere (living things).

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**21.1 Why use radiant flux intensity rather than total power to compare stars at different distances?** We cannot measure a star's total power directly; we only collect the small fraction that reaches Earth, i.e. its flux. Flux is what our instruments measure, and it depends on distance. Comparing fluxes, together with distances, lets us work out and compare true luminosities fairly.

**21.2 Role of standard candles; why is known luminosity critical?** They are cosmic "measuring tapes". The distance formula d = √(L/4πF) needs L. If L were not known accurately, every distance calculated from it would be wrong, and so would our measurements of the size and expansion rate of the universe.

**21.3 Why do spectral lines of distant galaxies appear at longer wavelengths? What does it show?** This redshift happens because the galaxies are moving away from us: as space expands, it stretches the light waves travelling through it. The larger the redshift, the faster the galaxy recedes, and the farther away it is. So the universe is expanding.

**21.4 How would galaxy spectra look if the universe were not expanding?** The spectral lines would appear at their normal laboratory wavelengths, with no systematic redshift. Individual galaxies would show only small random shifts (some red, some blue) from their own motion, with no link between shift and distance.

**21.5 How does solar energy shape climate, and how does the equator–pole imbalance drive circulation?** Sunlight is the energy source for weather and climate. The equator receives direct, intense sunlight; the poles receive slanting, weak sunlight. Warm air rises at the equator (low pressure) and cold air sinks at the poles (high pressure), so air flows between them. Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect) bends these flows into the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies, forming the Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells. Together with ocean currents, this moves heat from the tropics towards the poles.

**21.6 How do human activities create an imbalance in Earth's energy budget?** Burning fossil fuels and cutting forests increase greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane) in the air. These absorb outgoing infrared radiation and send part of it back down, so less energy escapes to space than arrives from the Sun. The extra energy warms the planet (global warming), melting ice and reducing reflection, which adds further warming.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**21.1 Flux at Earth from a star of power 3.6 × 10²⁶ W at 1.5 × 10¹¹ m.**

$$
F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{3.6\times10^{26}}{4\pi(1.5\times10^{11})^2} = 1.3\times10^{3}\ \text{W m}^{-2}
$$

**21.2 Distance of a supernova.** L = 1.0 × 10³⁶ W, F = 2.0 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻².

$$
d = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi F}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.0\times10^{36}}{4\pi(2.0\times10^{-12})}} = 2\times10^{23}\ \text{m}
$$

**21.3 Surface temperature from a 500 nm peak.**

$$
T = \frac{b}{\lambda_{max}} = \frac{2.9\times10^{-3}}{500\times10^{-9}} = 5800\ \text{K}
$$

**21.4 Luminosity of a star with r = 7.0 × 10⁸ m and T = 6000 K.**

$$
L = 4\pi r^2\sigma T^4 = 4\pi(7.0\times10^{8})^2(5.67\times10^{-8})(6000)^4 = 4.5\times10^{26}\ \text{W}
$$

**21.5 Radius of a star with L = 3.9 × 10²⁶ W and T = 5800 K.**

$$
r = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.9\times10^{26}}{4\pi(5.67\times10^{-8})(5800)^4}} = 7.0\times10^{8}\ \text{m}
$$

**21.6 Redshift of a line moved from 656 nm to 700 nm.**

$$
z = \frac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0} = \frac{700 - 656}{656} = 0.067
$$

Redshift is a pure number with no unit (the book prints "m" by mistake).

**21.7 Speed of a galaxy with z = 0.1.**

$$
v = zc = 0.1 \times 3\times10^{8} = 3\times10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1} = 30\,000\ \text{km s}^{-1}
$$

**21.8 Distance of a galaxy receding at 2.1 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ (H₀ = 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹).** Convert the speed: 2.1 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ = 2100 km s⁻¹.

$$
d = \frac{v}{H_0} = \frac{2100}{70} = 30\ \text{Mpc} = 30 \times 3.086\times10^{22} = 9.26\times10^{23}\ \text{m}
$$
