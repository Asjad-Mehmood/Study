This chapter explains AC (current that keeps reversing), how resistors, inductors and capacitors behave in AC circuits, resonance, rectification (AC to DC), and self and mutual induction. The key words are rms value, reactance, impedance, phase, and resonance.

**How to learn this chapter (about 4 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** AC terms (peak, peak-to-peak, average, rms), mean power = ½ peak power, phase lead and lag.
2. **Session 2:** AC through R, L and C. Memory hook **"ELI the ICE man"**: in an inductor (L), voltage E comes before current I (ELI); in a capacitor (C), current I comes before voltage E (ICE). Learn X\_L = 2πfL and X\_C = 1/2πfC.
3. **Session 3:** RC, RL and RLC circuits, impedance triangles, resonance f = 1/2π√(LC).
4. **Session 4:** rectifiers and smoothing, self and mutual induction, energy in an inductor. Then MCQs and numericals.

### 1. Key ideas

**Alternating current (AC)**: current or voltage that varies like a sine wave: V = V₀ sin ωt, I = I₀ sin ωt. It reverses direction every half cycle. The two halves are mirror images, and the peak value stays the same in every cycle. AC is preferred for power supply because it can be sent long distances (between cities) with little energy loss, using transformers.

**Terms used with AC**

- **Waveform:** the shape of the graph of the AC quantity against time.
- **Cycle:** one complete set of positive and negative values.
- **Time period (T)** and **frequency (f = 1/T)**: Pakistan's mains supply is 50 Hz.
- **Instantaneous value:** value at any moment, V = V₀ sin 2πft.
- **Peak value (V₀, I₀):** the highest value reached in a cycle (the amplitude).
- **Peak-to-peak value:** from the positive peak to the negative peak, V\_pp = 2V₀.
- **Average (mean) value:** over a full cycle it is zero, so we take it over a half cycle: V\_av = 0.637V₀. It matters in rectifiers.
- **rms (effective) value:** the steady DC value that would produce the same heating (power) in a resistor. V\_rms = V₀/√2 = 0.707V₀. Meters and appliance ratings show rms values; "220 V mains" is 220 V rms (peak about 311 V).

**Mean power in a resistor**: the instantaneous power I²R swings between zero and the peak P\_max = I₀²R. Its average over a cycle is exactly half: P\_mean = ½P\_max = I\_rms²R = V\_rms I\_rms.

**Phase, lead and lag**: the phase is the angle θ = ωt that fixes the instantaneous value. If current reaches its peak before the voltage, the current **leads**; if after, it **lags**. The angle between them is the phase difference φ. An AC quantity can be drawn as a **phasor**: an arrow rotating anticlockwise at ω, whose length is the peak or rms value.

**AC through each component**

| Component | Opposition | Phase between V and I | Power used | DC vs high-frequency AC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor R | Resistance R = V/I | In phase (0°) | Yes: I²R as heat | Same for both |
| Inductor L | Inductive reactance X\_L = 2πfL | Current lags voltage by 90° | Zero (stores and returns energy) | Passes DC (X\_L = 0), blocks high f |
| Capacitor C | Capacitive reactance X\_C = 1/2πfC | Current leads voltage by 90° | Zero (stores and returns energy) | Blocks DC (X\_C = ∞), passes high f |

**Inductor**: a coil with large self-inductance and small resistance. A changing current makes a back emf that opposes the change, so the current lags.

**Choke**: a coil of thick copper wire with many turns on a laminated soft-iron core. Its resistance is tiny and its reactance large, so its power factor is almost zero and it limits AC current while wasting almost no power. Uses: filtering high-frequency noise in power supplies, stopping radio-frequency leakage, starting fluorescent tubes, limiting current surges.

**Capacitor in AC**: no charge crosses the gap, but the plates charge one way, then the other, so electrons flow back and forth in the wires: AC passes.

**Impedance (Z)**: the total opposition in an AC circuit, combining resistance and reactance: Z = V\_rms/I\_rms, in ohms. Resistance and reactance add like vectors (at right angles), not simply.

**RC and RL series circuits**: the voltage across R is in phase with I; the voltage across C lags by 90° (across L leads by 90°). So Z = √(R² + X²). The phase angle is θ = tan⁻¹(X/R). Only the resistor uses power: P = I²R = VI cosθ. **cosθ is the power factor.**

**RLC series circuit**: X\_L and X\_C act in opposite directions, so Z = √(R² + (X\_L − X\_C)²).

- At high frequency X\_L dominates, so the circuit acts like RL. At low frequency X\_C dominates, so it acts like RC.
- **Resonance**: at one frequency X\_L = X\_C and they cancel. Then Z = R (minimum), current is maximum, V and I are in phase, power factor = 1. f\_r = 1/2π√(LC).
- At resonance the voltages across L and C separately can be much bigger than the supply voltage (voltage magnification), though they cancel each other.
- A larger R gives more damping and a flatter resonance (like a car's shock absorber).
- Uses: tuning radios, band-pass filters.

**RLC parallel circuit**: same voltage across all three. At resonance (X\_L = X\_C), impedance is **maximum** and source current is **minimum**, while large currents circulate between L and C (current magnification). f\_r = 1/2π√(LC). Admittance Y = 1/Z. Quality factor Q measures sharpness of resonance. Uses: tuning, signal processing, induction cooktops.

**Inverter efficiency**: a solar panel gives DC; an inverter changes it to AC with some loss. P\_AC = efficiency × P\_DC (efficiency usually 85–90%).

**Rectification**: changing AC into DC using diodes (which conduct only when forward biased).

- **Half-wave rectifier (one diode):** passes only the positive half cycles; the negative halves are blocked. Output is pulsating DC with gaps.
- **Full-wave bridge rectifier (four diodes):** in the positive half cycle, one pair of diodes conducts; in the negative half, the other pair conducts. Current through the load always flows the same way, so both halves are used. Used in mobile phone chargers, laptops and UPS systems.
- **Smoothing capacitor:** connected in parallel with the load. It charges as the voltage rises and discharges as it falls, filling in the dips to give nearly steady DC. Ripple voltage V\_ripple = I\_load/(2fC): a bigger C or a smaller load current (larger load resistance) gives less ripple. Ripple frequency is 2f (100 Hz for 50 Hz mains).

**Self-induction**: a changing current in a coil induces an emf in the **same coil**, opposing the change. Self-inductance L = NΦ/I. ε = −L(ΔI/Δt). Unit: henry (H): 1 V induced when current changes at 1 A s⁻¹. L depends on the number of turns, cross-section area, and core material (an iron core increases it greatly).

**Energy stored in an inductor**: the battery does work against the back emf while current builds up. This is stored in the magnetic field: U = ½LI². Energy density u = B²/2μ₀.

**Mutual induction**: a changing current in one coil (primary) induces an emf in a **nearby coil** (secondary). M = N\_sΦ\_s/I\_p and ε\_s = −M(ΔI\_p/Δt). Unit: henry. M depends on the turns of both coils, their areas, the core material and permeability, the distance between them, and their orientation. Uses: transformers, motors, generators, pacemakers, metal detectors.

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Mean power = ½ maximum power**

1. Instantaneous power in R: P = I²R = I₀²R sin²ωt. Its peak is P\_max = I₀²R.
2. The average of sin²ωt over a cycle is ½, so P\_mean = ½I₀²R.
3. Since I\_rms = I₀/√2, I\_rms² = I₀²/2. So:

$$
P_{mean} = I_{rms}^2 R = \frac{I_0^2 R}{2} = \frac{P_{max}}{2}
$$

**(b) Inductor in AC: X\_L = ωL, current lags by 90°**

1. Let I = I₀ sin ωt flow through a pure inductor. The back emf is L(ΔI/Δt), and the applied voltage must equal it.
2. The rate of change of I₀ sin ωt is I₀ω cos ωt, so:

$$
V = L\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = \omega L I_0 \cos\omega t = V_0 \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \quad V_0 = \omega L I_0
$$

3. The voltage is ahead by π/2, so the **current lags the voltage by 90°**.
4. Reactance = V\_rms/I\_rms = V₀/I₀:

$$
X_L = \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \omega L = 2\pi f L
$$

5. X\_L rises with f; for DC (f = 0), X\_L = 0. Average power is zero because energy stored in the magnetic field is returned to the source every half cycle.

**(c) Capacitor in AC: X\_C = 1/ωC, current leads by 90°**

1. Apply V = V₀ sin ωt. At every instant q = CV = CV₀ sin ωt.
2. Current is the rate of flow of charge:

$$
I = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t} = \omega C V_0 \cos\omega t = I_0 \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \quad I_0 = \omega C V_0
$$

3. The current is ahead by π/2, so the **current leads the voltage by 90°**.
4. Reactance:

$$
X_C = \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}
$$

5. For DC (f = 0), X\_C is infinite: a capacitor blocks DC. At high frequency X\_C is small, so AC passes easily.

**(d) Impedance of RC and RL series circuits**

1. The same current I\_rms flows through both parts. V\_R = I\_rms R is in phase with I; V\_C = I\_rms X\_C lags I by 90° (for an inductor, V\_L = I\_rms X\_L leads I by 90°).
2. These two voltages are at right angles, so add them like perpendicular vectors (Pythagoras):

$$
V_{rms} = \sqrt{V_R^2 + V_C^2} = I_{rms}\sqrt{R^2 + X_C^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z = \sqrt{R^2 + X_C^2}, \quad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{X_C}{R}
$$

$$
Z_{RL} = \sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L)^2}, \quad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{\omega L}{R}
$$

**(e) Resonant frequency of an RLC circuit**: at resonance X\_L = X\_C:

$$
\omega_r L = \frac{1}{\omega_r C} \Rightarrow \omega_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \Rightarrow f_r = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}
$$

**(f) Self-inductance and energy stored in an inductor**

1. Flux linkage NΦ ∝ I, so NΦ = LI. Faraday's law gives ε = −Δ(NΦ)/Δt = −L ΔI/Δt.
2. As the current rises from 0 to I in time Δt, the battery moves charge Δq = (average current) × Δt = (I/2)Δt against the back emf ε = LI/Δt.
3. Work done:

$$
W = \varepsilon\,\Delta q = \frac{LI}{\Delta t}\cdot\frac{I}{2}\Delta t = \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2
$$

4. For a solenoid, L = μ₀n²Aℓ and B = μ₀nI. Substituting gives U = (B²/2μ₀)(Aℓ), so the energy density is u = B²/2μ₀.

**(g) Mutual inductance**: flux linking the secondary is proportional to the primary current: N\_sΦ\_s = MI\_p. By Faraday's law, ε\_s = −Δ(N\_sΦ\_s)/Δt = −M ΔI\_p/Δt. So M = ε\_s / (ΔI\_p/Δt). One henry: a change of 1 A s⁻¹ in the primary induces 1 V in the secondary.

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**17.1 What impact does resistance have on capacitance?** None on the value of C itself, which depends only on plate area, separation and dielectric. But R controls how fast the capacitor charges and discharges (τ = RC). In AC, R and X\_C together set the impedance and the phase angle.

**17.2 What happens when an AC line touches a DC line?** The AC voltage adds onto the DC line. DC equipment then receives a varying voltage it was not built for, peaks may exceed its rating, and large currents can flow. Results: blown fuses, burnt components, damaged batteries and electronics, sparks and fire risk.

**17.3 Peak-to-peak vs amplitude.** Amplitude (peak value V₀) is measured from zero to the top of the wave. Peak-to-peak is from the top to the bottom: V\_pp = 2V₀. For a 220 V rms supply, V₀ ≈ 311 V and V\_pp ≈ 622 V.

**17.4 Why are RC circuits used in timing circuits?** A capacitor charges and discharges through a resistor in a predictable time set by τ = RC. Choosing R and C sets the delay exactly. Examples: the start-up delay in refrigerators and ACs, blinking lights, and timer circuits.

**17.5 Why is a choke preferred to a resistor for limiting AC?** A resistor limits current by turning energy into heat (I²R loss). A choke limits current by its reactance X\_L; since its resistance is tiny and V and I are about 90° apart, it uses almost no power. So the choke saves energy and stays cool.

**17.6 Define rms voltage and current, with phasors.** rms current is the steady direct current that produces the same power in a resistor as the AC; similarly for rms voltage. I\_rms = I₀/√2, V\_rms = V₀/√2. In a phasor diagram, each is drawn as an arrow whose length equals its rms (or peak) value, rotating anticlockwise at ω; for a resistor, the V and I arrows point the same way.

**17.7 Four applications of resonance in LRC circuits.** (i) Tuning radio and TV receivers to a station. (ii) Band-pass filters in communication devices. (iii) Induction cooktops and induction heating. (iv) Oscillators that generate signals; also metal detectors and wireless chargers.

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**17.1 What is meant by the rating of an appliance?** The values marked on it for safe, normal working: voltage, power, and often current and frequency (e.g. "220 V, 50 Hz, 1000 W"). These are rms values. At a lower voltage the appliance works poorly; at a higher voltage it may overheat or burn out. The rating also tells us the current it draws (I = P/V) so we choose the right wire and fuse.

**17.2 Energy in an inductor is like the K.E. of a moving mass. Justify.** U = ½LI² has the same form as K.E. = ½mv². Inductance L plays the role of mass: mass resists a change in velocity (inertia), and L resists a change in current (back emf). Current plays the role of velocity. A moving mass stores energy and gives it back when it slows down; an inductor stores energy in its field and gives it back when the current falls.

**17.3 Safety measures with AC or DC.** Switch off the supply at the main breaker before working. Use insulated tools, rubber gloves and dry footwear; keep hands dry. Make sure appliances are earthed, and use correct fuses, circuit breakers and RCDs. Never overload sockets or touch bare wires. Discharge large capacitors before handling. For DC, check polarity and never short-circuit battery terminals.

**17.4 Voltages across L and C can be much larger than the source voltage. Justify.** (This happens at or near resonance.) At resonance Z = R is small, so the current I = V/R is large. Then V\_L = IX\_L and V\_C = IX\_C can each be many times the supply voltage. They do not break the rules because V\_L and V\_C are 180° apart and cancel; the supply only has to provide V\_R. This is called voltage magnification. Far off resonance, the current is smaller, so the effect is weaker.

**17.5 How does self-induction show up when a current is switched on or off?** On: the back emf opposes the rising current, so the current grows gradually, not instantly. Off: the current falls very fast, so a large emf is induced that tries to keep it flowing; this causes a spark across the switch. This effect is used to light fluorescent tubes and in car ignition coils.

**17.6 Can mutual inductance occur with four coils?** Yes. Mutual inductance exists between any two coils whose magnetic flux links. With four coils there is a mutual inductance for each pair (6 pairs). Example: a transformer with one primary and three secondary windings on the same core; a changing primary current induces an emf in all three.

**17.7 About 0.1 A through the body can be fatal. Is it heating or something else?** Mainly something else. The heart beats by small electrical signals. A current of about 0.1 A through the chest disturbs these signals and causes ventricular fibrillation (the heart quivers instead of pumping). It can also paralyse the breathing muscles. Heating and burns matter only at much larger currents.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**17.1 R = 20 Ω and C = 40 μF in series on 110 V, 50 Hz.**

$$
X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C} = \frac{1}{2\pi(50)(40\times10^{-6})} = 79.58\ \Omega \qquad Z = \sqrt{20^2 + 79.58^2} = 82.05\ \Omega
$$

$$
I = \frac{V}{Z} = \frac{110}{82.05} = 1.34\ \text{A}
$$

(c) In a purely capacitive circuit the current leads the voltage by 90°. In this RC circuit it leads by tan⁻¹(79.58/20) = 75.9°.

**17.2 I = 420 sin(100πt).** I₀ = 420 A and ω = 100π rad s⁻¹.

$$
I_{rms} = 0.707 \times 420 = 296.9\ \text{A} \qquad f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{100\pi}{2\pi} = 50\ \text{Hz}
$$

**17.3 Mean induced voltage in the secondary.** f = 50 Hz, I₀ = 1 A, M = 1.5 H. The current rises from 0 to its peak in a quarter cycle: T/4 = (1/50)/4 = 0.005 s.

$$
\varepsilon_{mean} = M\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = 1.5 \times \frac{1 - 0}{0.005} = 300\ \text{V}
$$

The book prints 3000 V, but the given data give 300 V (the same result comes from (2/π) × MωI₀ = 0.637 × 471 V). Check with your teacher.

**17.4 Capacitor of 10 μF on 220 V, 2 kHz.**

$$
X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi(2000)(10\times10^{-6})} = 7.96\ \Omega \qquad I = \frac{V}{X_C} = \frac{220}{7.96} = 27.6\ \text{A}
$$

**17.5 Induced emf in a 150 mH coil.** Current falls from 50 mA to 30 mA in 164 ms.

$$
|\varepsilon| = L\frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = 0.150 \times \frac{0.020}{0.164} = 1.829\times10^{-2}\ \text{V} = 18.29\ \text{mV}
$$

(b) The current is decreasing, so the induced emf tries to keep it going (Lenz's law): it acts **in the same direction as the original current**.

**17.6 Choke for a lamp.** Supply 160 V rms, 50 Hz; lamp R = 5 Ω at 10 A. Total impedance needed: Z = V/I = 160/10 = 16 Ω.

$$
X_L = \sqrt{Z^2 - R^2} = \sqrt{16^2 - 5^2} = 15.2\ \Omega \qquad L = \frac{X_L}{2\pi f} = \frac{15.2}{2\pi(50)} = 48.4\ \text{mH}
$$
