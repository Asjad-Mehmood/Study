This chapter shows how physics lets doctors see inside the body: ultrasound (using the piezoelectric effect), X-rays, CT scans and PET scans, plus radiotherapy for cancer. For each method, learn three things: what it uses, how the image is made, and what it is best for.

**How to learn this chapter (about 2 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** ionizing vs non-ionizing radiation; piezoelectric effect and ultrasound; X-ray production (characteristic and continuous) with λ\_min = hc/eV.
2. **Session 2:** X-ray contrast and attenuation, CT, PET, radiotherapy. Then the comparison table below, MCQs and the 4 numericals.
3. **Memory hook:** "Ultra-Sound, X-Ray, CT-Slice, PET-Pair": ultrasound uses sound echoes, X-rays use absorption, CT uses slices, PET uses photon pairs.

### 1. Key ideas

**Medical physics** applies physics to diagnosing and treating disease: imaging inside the body without surgery, finding fractures and tumours, and treating cancer safely.

**Radiation in medicine**

- **Ionizing:** X-rays, gamma rays, positrons (from tracers). They can knock electrons out of atoms. X-rays and gamma rays interact with matter by the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering.
- **Non-ionizing:** ultrasound and radio waves (used in MRI). They are reflected, refracted or absorbed, and are safer.

**Piezoelectric effect** (Pierre and Jacques Curie, 1880; from the Greek *piezein*, "to press"): certain crystals (quartz, tourmaline, Rochelle salt, and ceramics like PZT) have no centre of symmetry.

- **Direct effect:** pressing or squeezing the crystal shifts its internal charges, so a voltage appears across it (pressure → electricity).
- **Converse effect:** applying a voltage makes the crystal change shape slightly (electricity → movement). An alternating voltage makes it vibrate and send out sound.

**Ultrasound transducer**: one piezoelectric crystal does both jobs. It sends a short pulse of ultrasound (converse effect), then switches to listening and turns returning echoes into electrical signals (direct effect).

**How an ultrasound image is made**

1. The probe sends pulses of 1–15 MHz into the body.
2. At each boundary between tissues with different **acoustic impedance** (Z = density × speed of sound), part of the wave reflects as an echo.
3. The transducer receives the echoes. The computer uses the **time delay** to work out depth (depth = speed × time ÷ 2, since the pulse goes there and back; speed in soft tissue ≈ 1540 m s⁻¹) and the **strength** of each echo to set brightness.
4. Strong echoes show as bright areas, building a real-time picture.

**Factors affecting ultrasound image quality**

- **Impedance difference:** a bigger difference (e.g. soft tissue and bone) gives stronger echoes and clearer edges.
- **Frequency:** higher frequency gives better detail but less depth; lower frequency goes deeper but is less sharp.
- **Gel:** removes air between probe and skin, because air would reflect almost all the ultrasound.
- The Doppler effect lets ultrasound measure blood flow speed.

**Uses of ultrasound**: pregnancy (baby's growth, age, abnormalities); abdomen (liver, kidneys, gallbladder, pancreas, bladder); heart (echocardiography); urology (prostate, urinary system); muscles and joints (ligament injuries, inflammation). It is safe, non-invasive and can be repeated, since there is no ionizing radiation.

**X-rays**: high-energy photons from **inner-shell** electron transitions in heavy atoms. Electrons in heavy atoms sit in shells K, L, M, N … (K is closest to the nucleus). Inner electrons are tightly bound, so transitions between inner shells release large energies.

**Production (X-ray tube)**: a heated filament (cathode) gives off electrons. A high voltage V accelerates them to the tungsten target (anode), so each arrives with K.E. = eV. Cooling channels remove the heat. Two kinds of X-rays come out:

- **Characteristic X-rays:** a fast electron knocks a K-shell electron out. An electron from a higher shell drops into the hole and emits a photon. L → K gives **Kα**, M → K gives **Kβ**, N → K gives **Kγ** (each higher in energy). Their energies are fixed by the target element, so they appear as sharp peaks.
- **Continuous X-rays (bremsstrahlung, "braking radiation"):** electrons are suddenly slowed by the target, and decelerating charges emit radiation. They lose different amounts of energy, giving a continuous spread. An electron losing all its energy in one go gives the highest-energy photon: eV = hf\_max, so there is a sharp cut-off at λ\_min = hc/eV.

**X-ray imaging and contrast**: X-rays lose intensity (attenuation) as they pass through the body: I = I₀e^(−μx). Dense tissue with high-Z atoms (bone, calcium) absorbs much more than soft tissue, so bone looks white and soft tissue grey. **Contrast** is how clearly different tissues can be told apart. It depends on the density and thickness of tissues and on the X-ray energy:

- **Hard X-rays** (high energy) for bones; **soft X-rays** (low energy) for soft tissue such as the breast.
- **Contrast media** with high atomic numbers, such as iodine (Z = 53) or barium (Z = 56), are swallowed or injected to make soft organs (gut, blood vessels) show up.
- **Image intensifiers** brighten real-time X-ray images (fluoroscopy), reducing the dose needed.

**CT scan (computed tomography)**: an ordinary X-ray is a flat 2-D picture in which front and back structures overlap (e.g. front and back ribs). In CT, the X-ray tube rotates around the patient, taking many images of one thin section from different angles. A computer combines them into a 2-D cross-section (slice). The table moves a little and the next slice is taken. All the slices are stacked to make a 3-D image. *Tomos* is Greek for "slice"; it is also called computed axial tomography. Invented by Hounsfield and Cormack in the early 1970s (Nobel Prize 1979).

- Advantages: 3-D images; shows size, shape and exact position of organs and tumours; separates tissues of similar density; detects strokes within minutes; guides radiotherapy.
- Drawback: it uses ionizing X-rays. A typical dose is about one-third of a year's natural background radiation (about four long-distance flights), so extra care is taken with pregnant women.

**SPECT**: a gamma-emitting tracer is injected; a rotating gamma camera records many views, which are reconstructed into 3-D images of blood flow and organ function.

**PET scan (positron emission tomography)**

1. A small amount of positron-emitting tracer is injected into a vein.
2. Blood carries it, and it collects more in very active tissue (many cancers, active parts of the brain and heart).
3. Each positron travels a very short distance, meets an electron and **annihilates**, producing two 0.51 MeV gamma photons travelling in opposite directions.
4. A ring of detectors around the patient picks up the two photons at the same moment (**coincidence detection**). The event lies on the line joining the two detectors.
5. A computer builds an image; brighter areas mean more tracer, i.e. more active tissue.

PET shows **function** (metabolism, chemistry), not just structure, so it can find cancer early and stage it, check brain function and spot heart problems before structural changes appear.

**Cancer treatment (radiotherapy)**: high-energy radiation kills tumour cells while sparing healthy tissue as much as possible.

- **Teletherapy:** the beam comes from a machine outside the body.
- **Brachytherapy:** a radioactive source is placed inside or next to the tumour.
- Medical physicists check equipment, plan treatment, calculate treatment times and map the dose, working with oncologists and dosimetrists.

**Comparison of imaging methods**

| Method | Uses | Image made from | Ionizing? | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ultrasound | Sound waves 1–15 MHz | Echoes from tissue boundaries | No | Soft tissue, pregnancy, heart, abdomen |
| X-ray | X-ray photons | Different absorption by tissues | Yes | Bones, fractures, chest |
| CT scan | X-rays from many angles + computer | Reconstructed slices stacked into 3-D | Yes (more dose) | Detailed 3-D anatomy, tumours, strokes |
| PET scan | Positron tracer → gamma pairs | Location of annihilation events | Yes | Function and metabolism: cancer, brain, heart |

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Cut-off (minimum) wavelength of X-rays**

1. An electron accelerated through V gains K.E. = eV.
2. The most energetic X-ray photon is produced when the electron loses all of this energy in a single collision: hf\_max = eV.
3. Since f = c/λ:

$$
\frac{hc}{\lambda_{min}} = eV \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda_{min} = \frac{hc}{eV}
$$

4. A higher tube voltage gives a shorter cut-off wavelength (more penetrating X-rays).

**(b) Energy of a Kα photon**: the photon carries the energy difference between the L and K shells: hf\_Kα = E\_L − E\_K. Because E\_L and E\_K depend on the target element, each element has its own characteristic lines.

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**20.1 Which radiation is produced in a PET scan?** Gamma rays: two 0.51 MeV photons from each positron–electron annihilation, moving in opposite directions.

**20.2 Which effect lets ultrasound transducers produce sound waves?** The (converse) piezoelectric effect: an alternating voltage makes the crystal vibrate and emit ultrasound. The direct effect lets it detect the echoes.

**20.3 One body area examined with ultrasound.** The abdomen (liver, kidneys, gallbladder), or the womb during pregnancy.

**20.4 What causes contrast in X-ray images?** Different tissues absorb X-rays by different amounts, depending on their density, thickness and atomic number. Bone absorbs strongly (white); soft tissue absorbs less (grey); air absorbs least (dark).

**20.5 One major difference between a CT scan and a standard X-ray.** A standard X-ray gives one flat 2-D image with overlapping organs. CT takes many images from different angles and builds separate slices and a 3-D image, so organs do not overlap.

**20.6 What happens when ultrasound reaches a boundary between two tissues?** Part of the wave is reflected back as an echo and the rest passes on. The bigger the difference in acoustic impedance, the stronger the echo.

**20.7 What does CT stand for, and how does it improve imaging?** Computed Tomography. A computer combines X-ray images taken from many angles into thin cross-sectional slices and a 3-D picture, showing the exact size, shape and position of structures.

**20.8 What are Kα, Kβ and Kγ X-rays?** Characteristic X-rays emitted when a hole in the K-shell is filled: from the L-shell (Kα), from the M-shell (Kβ) or from the N-shell (Kγ). Their energies increase in that order: Kα < Kβ < Kγ.

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**20.1 What does a time difference in detecting the two gamma rays tell us?** If the annihilation happened exactly midway between two detectors, both photons arrive together. A small time difference means the event was closer to the detector that fired first. Its distance from the midpoint is about c × Δt / 2. This "time of flight" information pinpoints the position along the line and sharpens the image.

**20.2 Why is ultrasound poor for air-filled organs like the lungs?** The acoustic impedance of air is very different from that of tissue, so almost all the ultrasound is reflected at the tissue–air boundary; air also absorbs ultrasound strongly. Very little sound gets into the lungs to give useful echoes. (This is also why gel is used on the skin.)

**20.3 X-ray contrast between two tissues is too low. What happens, and what is the solution?** The tissues look almost the same shade of grey, so they cannot be told apart and a problem may be missed. Solutions: use a contrast medium such as barium (swallowed) or iodine (injected); use lower-energy (soft) X-rays for soft tissue; or use a CT scan.

**20.4 How does CT reduce the overlapping of organs seen in plain X-rays?** It images one thin slice of the body at a time, from many angles around it. The computer reconstructs only that slice, so structures in front of and behind it do not pile on top of each other. Stacking the slices gives a 3-D view.

**20.5 Possible medical cause of uneven tracer concentration in a PET brain scan.** Bright spots (high uptake) can mean very active tissue, such as a brain tumour or the source of seizures (epilepsy). Dark areas (low uptake) can mean damaged or dead tissue, as after a stroke, or reduced activity in conditions like Alzheimer's disease or dementia.

**20.6 Purpose of lead collimators in gamma-ray detectors.** Lead absorbs gamma rays strongly. Collimators let only photons travelling along the intended straight paths reach the detector and block stray and scattered photons arriving at angles. This makes the image sharper, reduces false events and shields the detectors.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

**20.1 Wavelength emitted when an electron drops from −3.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ J to −1.20 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.**

$$
\Delta E = (-3.5\times10^{-19}) - (-12.0\times10^{-19}) = 8.5\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{8.5\times10^{-19}} = 2.34\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 234\ \text{nm}
$$

**20.2 Minimum X-ray wavelength at 3000 V.**

$$
\lambda_{min} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{(1.6\times10^{-19})(3000)} = 4.14\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}
$$

**20.3 Energy gap for copper's K X-ray (λ = 1.377 × 10⁻¹⁰ m).**

$$
\Delta E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{1.377\times10^{-10}} = 1.44\times10^{-15}\ \text{J} = \frac{1.44\times10^{-15}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 9.03\ \text{keV}
$$

**20.4 Shortest bremsstrahlung wavelength at 40 kV.**

$$
\lambda_{min} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{(1.6\times10^{-19})(40\times10^{3})} = 3.1\times10^{-11}\ \text{m} = 0.31\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}
$$
