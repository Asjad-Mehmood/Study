This chapter shows that light behaves as a wave, using interference and diffraction. Three equations do almost all the work: fringe spacing Δy = λL/d, the grating equation d sinθ = nλ, and Bragg's law 2d sinθ = nλ.

**How to learn this chapter (about 2–3 study sessions)**

1. **Session 1:** wavefronts, Huygens' principle, and the three conditions for interference (same type, coherent, same direction). Say them aloud.
2. **Session 2:** Young's double slit. Learn the rule "bright = whole wavelengths, dark = half wavelengths", then derive Δy = λL/d twice from memory.
3. **Session 3:** thin films, Newton's rings, grating and Bragg. Then do the MCQs and the 7 numericals.
4. **Exam tip:** in fringe questions, first ask "what happens to λ, L and d?" Δy grows with λ and L, and shrinks with d.

### 1. Key ideas

**Nature of light, in short**: Newton (1672) said light is particles (corpuscles). Huygens (1678) said light is a wave. Young's double-slit experiment (1801) proved the wave nature. Maxwell (1860) showed light is an electromagnetic wave. Modern physics says light has a **dual nature**: it travels as a wave but interacts with matter as particles called photons.

**Wavefront**: an imaginary surface joining all points of a wave that are in the same phase. A **ray** is a line at right angles to the wavefront showing the direction of travel (and energy flow). Neighbouring wavefronts are one wavelength apart.

- **Plane wavefronts:** straight, e.g. light from distant stars, or from a source at the focus of a lens.
- **Circular wavefronts:** in two dimensions, e.g. ripples in a pond.
- **Spherical wavefronts:** in three dimensions, from a point source of sound or light. Far from the source they look plane.

**Huygens' principle**: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets that spread out with the wave's speed. After time t, the new wavefront is the surface touching all these wavelets (their envelope). It works for all waves: light, sound and water.

**Interference**: when two waves of the same wavelength overlap, they add up (principle of superposition).

- **Constructive interference:** waves arrive in phase (crest on crest). Path difference = 0, λ, 2λ … Result: bright light or loud sound.
- **Destructive interference:** waves arrive out of phase (crest on trough). Path difference = λ/2, 3λ/2 … Result: darkness or silence.

**Conditions for a clear interference pattern**

1. Waves of the same type (light with light, sound with sound).
2. **Coherent** sources: a constant phase difference, which needs the same frequency.
3. Moving in the same, or nearly the same, direction.

Equal amplitudes are not necessary, but they give the best contrast (darkest dark fringes).

**Why two lamps or two car headlights give no pattern**: they are independent sources. Their light comes in random bursts, so the phase difference changes millions of times a second. The pattern shifts too fast to see, so we see even illumination. The fix: split light from **one** source (a laser) using two slits, so the two slits are coherent.

**Interference of sound and microwaves**: two loudspeakers on the same signal generator give loud and quiet spots in a room. Microwaves (λ = 2.8 cm) through two gaps in a metal sheet give strong and weak spots, found with a probe receiver.

**Young's double-slit experiment (1801)**: light from one source passes through two narrow slits. The light spreads out (diffracts) from each slit and overlaps on a screen, giving equally spaced bright and dark **fringes**.

- At the centre, both paths are equal: path difference 0, so a bright fringe (the central maximum).
- First dark fringe: path difference λ/2.
- Next bright fringe: path difference λ.
- Cover one slit and the fringes disappear, leaving just a broad band of light. This proves light reaches each point from both slits.

**Thin-film interference**: light reflects from both the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film (soap bubble, oil on water). The two reflected rays interfere. Different colours interfere constructively at different thicknesses, so we see coloured bands. The patterns follow the changes in film thickness.

**Air wedge**: two glass plates touching at one edge trap a thin wedge of air. Path difference = 2t (t = air thickness). Surprisingly, the line of contact is dark. This is because light reflecting from the denser glass below gets an extra half-wavelength (180°) phase shift.

**Newton's rings**: a convex lens resting on flat glass traps a thin air film that gets thicker outwards. In monochromatic light (e.g. a sodium lamp) this gives circular bright and dark rings, with a **dark centre** (because of the same 180° phase shift). Uses: finding wavelength, finding a lens's radius of curvature, and checking surfaces for flatness.

**Diffraction**: the bending and spreading of waves around obstacles and through gaps, into the geometrical shadow. Diffraction is strongest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength.

**Diffraction grating**: a plate with many equally spaced parallel slits (lines), often about 1000 per mm. Grating spacing d = 1/N, where N = lines per metre. Compared with two slits, a grating gives maxima at the same angles but much **sharper and brighter**, so wavelength can be measured very precisely.

- The central bright line is the **zero order** (n = 0); then come first-order (n = ±1), second-order (n = ±2) lines, and so on.
- With white light, each order is a full spectrum. Red (long λ) bends more than violet (short λ).

**X-ray diffraction (Bragg)**: X-rays have λ about 10⁻¹⁰ m, too small for a ruled grating. But the regular rows of atoms in a crystal (spacing about 10⁻¹⁰ m) act as a natural grating. W.H. Bragg and W.L. Bragg (1914) treated atomic planes as mirrors. The ray from the lower plane travels an extra 2d sinθ. Reinforcement happens when this equals nλ: **Bragg's law**. Uses: finding crystal spacing, and the structures of haemoglobin and DNA (the double helix).

| Quantity | Formula | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Path difference (two slits) | Δ = d sinθ | d = slit separation |
| Bright fringe | d sinθ = mλ | m = 0, 1, 2 … |
| Dark fringe | d sinθ = (m + ½)λ | m = 0, 1, 2 … |
| Position of mth bright fringe | y\_m = mλL / d | small angles; L = slit–screen distance |
| Fringe spacing | Δy = λL / d | same for bright and dark fringes |
| Grating spacing | d = 1/N | N = lines per metre |
| Grating equation | d sinθ = nλ | n = order |
| Highest order possible | n\_max = d/λ (whole number below it) | since sinθ ≤ 1 |
| Bragg's law | 2d sinθ = nλ | d = spacing of atomic planes; θ = glancing angle |
| Thin air wedge | path difference = 2t | plus a λ/2 shift on reflection from glass |
| Wavelength in a medium | λ\_medium = λ / n | n = refractive index |

### 3. Derivations

**(a) Fringe spacing in Young's double-slit experiment: Δy = λL/d**

1. Slits S₁ and S₂ are a distance d apart. The screen is a distance L away, with L much larger than d, so rays to a point P on the screen are almost parallel.
2. The ray from S₂ travels farther. The path difference is d sinθ.
3. For a bright fringe, the path difference must be a whole number of wavelengths: d sinθ = mλ.
4. The fringe is at height y\_m from the centre, so tanθ = y\_m/L. For small θ, sinθ ≈ tanθ = y\_m/L.
5. Substitute:

$$
d\,\frac{y_m}{L} = m\lambda \quad\Rightarrow\quad y_m = \frac{m\lambda L}{d}
$$

6. The next bright fringe is at y\_(m+1) = (m+1)λL/d. The spacing is:

$$
\Delta y = y_{m+1} - y_m = \frac{\lambda L}{d}
$$

7. Conclusion: fringes are equally spaced. They spread out for longer λ or a farther screen, and crowd together when the slits are farther apart.

**(b) Grating equation: d sinθ = nλ.** Rays from neighbouring slits leave at angle θ. The path difference between neighbouring slits is d sinθ. A bright line appears when every slit's light arrives in phase, i.e. when this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths: d sinθ = nλ.

**(c) Bragg's law: 2d sinθ = nλ.** X-rays hit parallel atomic planes (spacing d) at a glancing angle θ. The ray reflected from the lower plane travels an extra distance d sinθ going in and d sinθ coming out, a total of 2d sinθ. For reinforcement this must be a whole number of wavelengths: 2d sinθ = nλ.

### 5. Short answer questions (book exercise)

**15.1 Can Huygens' principle be applied to sound and water waves?** Yes. It is a general rule for any wave. Every point on a sound or water wavefront also acts as a source of secondary wavelets, and the envelope gives the new wavefront. That is why sound bends round doorways and ripples spread past gaps.

**15.2 Why is no interference pattern produced by a car's two headlights?** The two lamps are independent, incoherent sources: their phase difference changes randomly and very quickly. They also give white light with many wavelengths. Any pattern changes too fast to see, so we see uniform light.

**15.3 Why do soap bubbles show interference colours but window glass does not?** A soap film is very thin, comparable to the wavelength of light, so the two reflected rays have a small path difference and interfere clearly. Window glass is thousands of wavelengths thick, so the patterns for all colours overlap and wash out into white.

**15.4 Young's apparatus submerged in water: effect on fringes?** In water the wavelength becomes λ/n = λ/1.33. Since Δy = λL/d, the fringe spacing decreases by a factor of 1.33. The fringes come **closer together** (and the pattern gets a little dimmer).

**15.5 Compare double-slit arrangements for light and sound.**

|  | Light | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sources | Two slits lit by one laser | Two loudspeakers on one signal generator |
| Wavelength | About 500 nm | About 0.1–1 m |
| Source spacing | Fraction of a mm | About 1 m |
| Detector | Screen (eye) | Microphone (ear) |
| Result | Bright and dark fringes | Loud and quiet spots |
| Wave type | Transverse, no medium needed | Longitudinal, needs air |

Same in both: coherent sources from one origin, and the same rule (whole λ = maximum, half λ = minimum).

**15.6 Advantages of (a) many slits, (b) narrow slits, (c) closely spaced slits in a grating.** (a) Many slits make the maxima very sharp and bright, so their angles are measured precisely. (b) Narrow slits spread (diffract) light widely, so it reaches the higher orders. (c) Closely spaced slits (small d) give larger angles, so the lines are well separated and easier to measure.

**15.7 How can the number of orders be increased?** n\_max = d/λ. Increase d (use a grating with fewer lines per mm) or use light of shorter wavelength.

**15.8 Red light replaced with blue light in a grating?** Blue has a shorter wavelength, so sinθ = nλ/d is smaller. The lines move **closer to the centre**, and more orders become visible.

**15.9 Why is the central spot in Newton's rings dark?** At the point of contact the air film thickness is zero, so the path difference is nearly zero. But the ray reflected at the air-to-glass surface (from the lower plate) undergoes a phase change of 180°, equal to an extra λ/2. So the two rays are out of phase and cancel, giving a dark centre.

### 6. Constructed response questions (book exercise)

**15.1 Light as rays vs waves (diagrams).** Draw two diagrams. Ray diagram: straight arrows from a lamp through a lens, used for reflection, refraction and images (geometrical optics). Wave diagram: curved wavefront lines (circles from a point source, straight lines far away) with rays drawn at right angles to them, used for interference and diffraction (physical optics). Rays show only direction; wavefronts also show phase and wavelength.

**15.2 One slit with a red filter, the other with a blue filter.** (Note: the book swaps the wavelengths; red is about 650 nm and blue about 400–450 nm.) The light from the two slits now has different wavelengths, so it is **not coherent**. No interference fringes form. The screen just shows the red and blue light from the two slits overlapping into an even purplish (magenta) patch.

**15.3 One slit made half as wide.** Less light comes through that slit, so the two waves have unequal amplitudes. The dark fringes are no longer completely dark and the bright fringes are less bright, so the **contrast drops**. The fringe width stays the same, because Δy = λL/d does not involve slit width.

**15.4 Why is diffraction easy to see for sound and water waves but not light? Is it because light is faster?** No, speed has nothing to do with it. Diffraction is noticeable when the gap is about the size of the wavelength. Sound (about 1 m) and water waves (cm) match the size of doors and gaps. Light's wavelength (about 500 nm) is far smaller than everyday openings, so it barely bends; we need very narrow slits to see it.

**15.5 Significance of path difference in deriving Bragg's equation.** The two X-ray beams reflected from neighbouring planes travel different distances; the extra distance is 2d sinθ. Whether the reflected X-rays reinforce or cancel depends entirely on this path difference. Setting it equal to nλ gives Bragg's law, which links the measurable angle θ to the crystal spacing d.

### 8. Numerical problems (solved)

The book gives no answers for these, so all answers here are worked out.

**15.1 Distance between the m = 49 and m = 50 maxima.** λ = 500 nm, L = 2.00 m, spacing near the centre = 3.53 cm. Near the centre Δy = λL/d, so:

$$
d = \frac{\lambda L}{\Delta y} = \frac{(500\times10^{-9})(2.00)}{0.0353} = 2.83\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}
$$

At m = 49 and 50 the angles are large, so the small-angle formula fails. Use sinθ = mλ/d, then y = L tanθ:

- m = 49: sinθ = 0.865, θ = 59.9°, y = 2.00 × tan 59.9° = 3.45 m
- m = 50: sinθ = 0.883, θ = 62.0°, y = 2.00 × tan 62.0° = 3.75 m
- **Distance = 3.75 − 3.45 = 0.31 m (about 31 cm)**, much more than 3.53 cm. Fringes spread out far from the centre.

**15.2 Slit separation.** λ = 502 nm, L = 1.20 m, the 20th bright fringe is at y = 10.6 mm.

$$
d = \frac{m\lambda L}{y_m} = \frac{20(502\times10^{-9})(1.20)}{10.6\times10^{-3}} = 1.14\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1.14\ \text{mm}
$$

**15.3 Slit separation from dark-fringe spacing.** λ = 450 nm, L = 1.80 m, Δy = 3.90 mm (dark fringes have the same spacing as bright ones).

$$
d = \frac{\lambda L}{\Delta y} = \frac{(450\times10^{-9})(1.80)}{3.90\times10^{-3}} = 2.08\times10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.208\ \text{mm}
$$

**15.4 Distance between the 2nd and 3rd dark lines.** d = 0.450 mm, L = 0.750 m, λ = 500 nm. Neighbouring dark lines are one fringe spacing apart.

$$
\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d} = \frac{(500\times10^{-9})(0.750)}{0.450\times10^{-3}} = 8.33\times10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.833\ \text{mm}
$$

**15.5 Distance between the 1st and 2nd dark lines.** d = 1.80 mm, L = 0.350 m, λ = 550 nm.

$$
\Delta y = \frac{(550\times10^{-9})(0.350)}{1.80\times10^{-3}} = 1.07\times10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.107\ \text{mm}
$$

**15.6 Fourth-order maximum.** The first order is at 11.3°. Since d sinθ = nλ, sinθ is proportional to n:

$$
\sin\theta_4 = 4\sin 11.3^\circ = 4(0.196) = 0.784 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_4 = 51.6^\circ
$$

**15.7 Second-order violet line.** First find d from the red line (n = 3, λ = 700 nm, θ = 65°):

$$
d = \frac{n\lambda}{\sin\theta} = \frac{3(700\times10^{-9})}{\sin 65^\circ} = 2.32\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}
$$

Then for violet (n = 2, λ = 400 nm):

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{2(400\times10^{-9})}{2.32\times10^{-6}} = 0.345 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = 20.2^\circ
$$
